# Programming

How hard, how much, how fast and how to progress with a home or hotel kit. Each claim gives what was found, its source and what it doesn't show; each section ends with the planning rule. Where this file and `me/health.md` or `knowledge/injury-playbook.md` disagree, the strictest wins.

## Effort

- **Failure isn't required.** Grgic and colleagues (2022, 15 studies, young adults) found no difference between training to failure and stopping short, for strength or for muscle size. Where the groups' volume wasn't matched, stopping short gave more strength. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33497853/ It doesn't cover older adults, and in the trained subgroup failure did favour muscle size (effect size 0.15, interval 0.03 to 0.26).
- **Close matters more for size than strength.** Refalo and colleagues (2023, 15 studies) found only a trivial muscle-size edge for set failure over stopping short (effect size 0.19, interval touching zero), and no edge for true momentary failure. They suggest ending most sets close to failure and keeping failure for the last set or simple movements. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9935748 It measured muscle size only, and most studies used untrained people.
- **The same pattern, continuous.** Robinson and colleagues (2024, meta-regressions) found strength gains were similar across a wide range of reps in reserve, while muscle size grew more as sets ended closer to failure. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/ The reps in reserve were estimated from study descriptions, and the authors call it exploratory.
- **Saying it by ear.** Zourdos and colleagues (2016, 29 squatters) tested a scale where effort ten means no reps left, nine means one left, and so on, and called it a practical way to set daily load. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/ It was tested on barbell squats; it doesn't show how well people judge reps left on light loads at home, and Refalo notes people tend to underestimate how close they are at lower loads.

Rule: end working sets with about one to three reps left ("effort eight or nine out of ten"), and take only the last set of a simple movement close to failure. Never cue failure on a movement an injury rule limits.

## Load range

- **Light loads build muscle, heavy loads build top strength.** Schoenfeld and colleagues (2017, 21 studies, every set to failure) found muscle growth similar for light (60 percent of one-rep max or less) and heavy loads, while one-rep max gains favoured heavy loads. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/ All sets went to failure; it doesn't show light loads match heavy ones when sets stop well short.
- **A wide rep range works.** Iversen and colleagues (2021, narrative review) recommend loads you can lift 6 to 15 times, and 15 to 40 reps if the set goes to or near failure. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772 It's a narrative review.

Rule: with light dumbbells, use higher reps (15 to 30) and take sets closer to failure than with a heavy weight. Don't promise one-rep strength from light kit; promise muscle and working strength.

## Weekly dose

- **More sets, more growth.** Schoenfeld and colleagues (2017, 15 studies) found each extra weekly set per muscle added a little more growth (about 0.37 percent). Grouped as under 5, 5 to 9 and 10 or more sets a week, the difference was only a trend. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/ Only two studies used trained people, and there was too little data at high volumes to find a ceiling.
- **Twice a week beats once.** Schoenfeld and colleagues (2016, 10 studies) found training a muscle twice a week grew it more than once, with volume matched. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27102172/ Three times against twice was left open.
- **A floor for busy weeks.** Iversen and colleagues advise at least four hard sets per muscle a week, say weekly volume matters more than how often, and note three multi-joint exercises can cover the major muscles. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772 Four sets is a floor for progress, not the most growth.
- **Frequency by experience.** The American College of Sports Medicine (2009 position stand) recommends two to three days a week for novices and three to four for intermediates. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/ It dates from 2009, for healthy adults.

What a session holds (house arithmetic, not a study): a set takes about two minutes with its rest, about half that when paired. Fifteen minutes holds about six hard sets; twenty-five about ten to twelve; forty-five about twenty.

Rule: aim for at least four hard sets per major muscle group a week, spread over two or more sessions, and more when the week allows. The coverage check spreads groups across sessions.

## Rest and pairing

- **Rest.** Iversen and colleagues found one to two minutes between sets is enough for untrained people; trained people probably need two minutes or more. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772 It's a narrative review, and most studies were on untrained or recreational lifters.
- **Pairing saves time.** The same review found supersets, drop sets and rest-pause roughly halve session time while keeping volume, and favoured supersets of different muscles, such as opposing ones. Evidence is limited, better for muscle than for top strength, and not advised for heavy free-weight squats and presses. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772
- **Rest by goal.** The American College of Sports Medicine recommends one to two minutes for muscle work and under 90 seconds for muscular endurance with light loads. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/ The ranges are expert consensus, not trial results.

Rule: pair opposing muscles (push with pull) or upper with lower, so one rests while the other works, and give about a minute between the two halves of a pair. Unpaired sets get one to two minutes.

## Muscle coverage and recovery

- **Frequency doesn't matter when weekly volume is the same.** Schoenfeld, Grgic and Krieger (2019, 25 studies) found no difference in muscle growth between training a muscle more or less often per week when weekly volume was matched, including in trained people and for upper and lower body; without matched volume, more frequent training grew muscle modestly more. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/ It measures growth, not strength, and says the frequency can follow personal preference.
- **The guideline asks for every major group, two to three days a week.** The American College of Sports Medicine (2011 position stand) recommends resistance exercise for each of the major muscle groups on two to three days a week. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/ Expert consensus for healthy adults; it doesn't fix how a session divides the groups.
- **The building response fades within a day and a half.** MacDougall and colleagues (1995, 6 young men, 12 heavy sets of arm curls with one arm) found muscle protein synthesis more than doubled at 24 hours and nearly back to baseline (within 14 percent) by about 36 hours. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8563679/ This is the growth signal in one small muscle, not how soon the muscle is ready to work hard again.

Rule: when the listener trains two or three times a week, each session touches every major muscle group; with more frequent sessions, full-body sessions are still fine, because spreading the same weekly sets thinly loses nothing. Avoid taking the same muscle close to failure on back-to-back days (house judgment; no source here sets the recovery time).

## Exercise order

- **What comes first gets stronger; size doesn't care.** Nunes and colleagues (2021, 11 studies of good-to-excellent quality) found strength gains largest in the exercises done first: multi-joint lifts gained more when they opened the session (effect size 0.32), single-joint lifts gained more when they did (effect size -0.58), and there was no overall strength difference across all tests. Muscle growth didn't differ by order (effect size 0.03). Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/ Small number of studies; it doesn't cover circuits or light home loads specifically.

Rule: put the multi-joint movements (squat, hinge, press, row) first, while fresh, unless the session's priority is a single-joint movement such as a rehab exercise, which then goes first. For muscle size alone, order is free.

## Tempo

- **A wide range works.** Schoenfeld and colleagues (2015, 8 studies, all to failure) found muscle growth similar for reps lasting 0.5 to 8 seconds; very slow reps (over 10 seconds) looked worse. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25601394/ Few controlled studies, and some mixed load changes with tempo.
- **Avoid super-slow.** Iversen and colleagues advise a somewhat faster cadence when time is short and avoiding reps of 10 seconds or more. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772 It's a narrative review, and most studies were on untrained or recreational lifters.

Rule: keep each rep between about two and eight seconds in total (a three-second lowering is fine); never cue a single rep longer than about eight seconds. A slower lowering is a way to make a light weight harder, not a goal in itself.

## Progression

- **When to add load.** The American College of Sports Medicine recommends raising the load 2 to 10 percent once you can do one or two reps more than the target. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/ A home kit often jumps much more than 10 percent between dumbbells.

The order of steps when load is capped is house policy: a few more reps, a slower lowering, a pause at the hard point, more range, one arm or one leg, and only then the next dumbbell. Change one variable at a time.

Rule: "too easy" moves the next session one step along that list; "too hard" moves one step back, or trims a set. Say in the audio what changed.

## Warm-up, finisher and cool-down

- **Warm-up.** Iversen and colleagues say a general warm-up shouldn't be prioritized when time is short, a specific warm-up (lighter sets of the movement) helps with heavy loads (over 80 percent of one-rep max), and stretching isn't needed unless flexibility is the goal. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772 It's about performance, not injury risk, and doesn't address someone returning from injury.
- **A specific warm-up didn't change working-set reps.** Ribeiro and colleagues (2014 crossover, 15 men, four sets to failure at 80 percent of one-rep max on bench press, squat and arm curl) found no difference in total reps or fatigue between no warm-up, a specific warm-up, an aerobic warm-up and both combined. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25153744/ One small study of men; it measured reps, not injury or a first heavy single, and it cuts against the performance benefit Iversen and colleagues describe.
- **Cool-down.** Van Hooren and Peake (2018 review) found an active cool-down doesn't improve next-day performance, prevent injury or reduce soreness, and doesn't change mood much, though it speeds heart and breathing recovery. Static stretching after exercise didn't reduce soreness. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5999142/ Most studies were on sport.
- **Slow breathing after exercise.** A 2025 study of 15 young men found six breaths a minute after ten minutes of cycling raised two heart-rate-variability measures more than normal breathing, a sign of faster calming of the nervous system; heart rate itself didn't differ. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40079612/ One short session, men only, and no lasting benefit shown.

Rule: keep the warm-up short and specific: easy movement, then a lighter first set of the main movements, as a rehearsal and a check that the movement feels right today (not a promise of more reps). Close with slow breathing because it calms and ends the session well, and never claim it prevents soreness or injury. The finisher is a short, simple burst (house policy, not a study) and stays inside the injury rules.

## One-sided training

- **Carry-over.** Manca and colleagues (2017, 31 trials, 785 people) found training one limb raised the untrained limb's strength by about 12 percent (upper limb 9 percent, lower limb 16 percent), largest with lowering-focused training. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28936703/ The authors found a high risk of bias, and it measures strength, not size.
- **During immobilization.** Hendy and colleagues (2012 review) found that early strength loss after immobilization is partly in the nervous system, and that two studies kept strength in an immobilized limb by training the free one. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21924681/ It gives no size of the effect.

Rule: train both sides by default. When an injury rules out one side, training the healthy side still helps the injured one a little; say so in the audio, and never present it as a replacement for the injured side's own rehab.

## Conditioning sources

Two studies support short conditioning blocks: Gillen and colleagues (2016) found three 20-second all-out cycle sprints within a ten-minute session, three times a week for 12 weeks, raised fitness (peak oxygen uptake up 19 percent) as much as 50 minutes of steady cycling. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27115137/ Small study (9 sedentary young men in the sprint group), warm-up and cool-down included; all-out sprints may not suit every listener. Jenkins and colleagues (2019) found three short stair climbs a day, three days a week for six weeks, raised fitness modestly in sedentary young adults. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30649897/ It shows fitness snacks help, not that ten minutes builds strength.

Session shapes live in `knowledge/sessions.toml`: each method's goals, length, levels, warm-up, cool-down and finisher shares, the patterns that fill its blocks, and the dose ranges for each level, every number marked as sourced or as a house default. `./sweatcast brief` prints the level's doses and the methods that fit today's goal, length, level and kit as guidance; the writer chooses the shape, rotating away from the last session's format.

## Rest between intervals

- **A full sprint empties the fast fuel, and it comes back slowly.** Bogdanis and colleagues (1995, 14 men, two 30-second all-out cycle sprints, muscle biopsies in 8) found phosphocreatine back to 65 percent of resting after 1.5 minutes and only 85.5 percent after 6 minutes, a half-time of about 57 seconds; peak and early power in the second sprint had still not returned to normal after 6 minutes. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1157744 Young men, lab cycle ergometer, truly all-out efforts; a home "sprint" is rarely that hard.
- **Recovery of the fuel tracks recovery of power.** Bogdanis and colleagues (1996, 8 men, two sprints 4 minutes apart) found phosphocreatine back to 78.7 percent of resting after 3.8 minutes, and how much came back correlated with how much power came back in the first 10 seconds of the next sprint (r = 0.84). Work in a second 30-second sprint was about 18 percent lower, with aerobic metabolism supplying about half its energy. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8964751/ Small sample; it shows the mechanism, not a best rest length.
- **Short sprints on short rest fade.** Gaitanos and colleagues (1993, 8 men) had ten 6-second all-out sprints with 30 seconds' rest (rest five times the work); by the tenth sprint, mean power had fallen to 73 percent of the first. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8226473/ It shows that a five-to-one rest still lets power fall across repeats; it doesn't say which ratio prevents it.
- **The protocol already cited uses generous rest.** Gillen and colleagues' three 20-second all-out sprints (see Conditioning sources) were separated by 2 minutes of easy cycling at 50 watts, rest six times the work. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27115137/ It chose that rest; it didn't test shorter ones.

Not found with a source: a best rest for hard-but-not-all-out efforts of 30 to 60 seconds, or for steady work broken into blocks. The ratios below for those are house defaults.

Rule: genuine all-out efforts of 10 to 30 seconds get at least four times their length in easy movement, and five or six times when the listener should hit the next one as hard (a 20-second sprint gets about 2 minutes); expect the later ones to fade and say so, and keep them few. Hard efforts of 30 to 60 seconds that are not all-out take about equal to double their length in rest (house default), and steady blocks need only a short breather.

## Effort by ear

- **The talk test marks the first threshold.** Reed and Pipe (2014 review) found that in healthy adults and people with heart disease, comfortable speech is likely possible below the ventilatory or lactate threshold and unlikely above it; the test gave moderate-to-vigorous intensities within ACSM and Canadian guidelines and was consistent across walking, jogging, cycling, elliptical and stair stepping. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25010379/ The authors say it may not be practical for high-intensity interval training.
- **The same holds in trained people, and maps onto perceived effort.** Rodríguez-Marroyo and colleagues (2013, 18 highly trained cyclists) found the first stage where riders could not talk comfortably matched the first ventilatory threshold, and the stage where they definitely could not talk matched the second (respiratory compensation) threshold, with perceived exertion about 4 and about 7 at the two thresholds (r = 0.79 and 0.88). Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23007491/ Cyclists on a lab test; the abstract does not name the exertion scale, though the values fit a 0-to-10 scale.
- **Perceived exertion can set intervals.** Ciolac and colleagues (2015 pilot, 8 young adults, 20 minutes of 1-minute treadmill intervals) found that intervals set by perceived exertion (hard to very hard, 15 to 17 on the 6-to-20 scale, alternating with very light to fairly light, 9 to 11) gave the same heart rates and speeds as intervals set at 85 and 50 percent of heart-rate reserve. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26028809/ A pilot of eight people on the 6-to-20 scale; translating it to a 0-to-10 scale is house arithmetic.

Rule: for steady work, cue by talk: "you can speak in sentences" is moderate, "only a few words at a time" is above the first threshold. For intervals, where the talk test doesn't fit, cue a 0-to-10 effort: about 7 to 8 for hard intervals, 9 to 10 only for genuine sprints, and 3 to 4 for the recovery between (house mapping, anchored on the studies above).

## Stretching dose

- **Weekly time and frequency matter more than session length.** Thomas and colleagues (2018 review with a quantitative synthesis of 23 studies) found all stretching types improved range of motion over weeks, static stretching more than ballistic or PNF; total weekly time mattered once it reached about 5 minutes per muscle, time within one session didn't, and more days a week helped. They suggest static stretching at least 5 days a week for at least 5 minutes a week. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506306/ A synthesis, not a formal meta-analysis; its recommendation is hedged ("may be beneficial").
- **Thirty seconds a hold is enough.** Bandy and colleagues (1997 trial, 93 adults with tight hamstrings, 5 days a week for 6 weeks) found a 30-second hold improved range of motion, and neither 60-second holds nor stretching three times a day did better than once. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9327823/ One muscle group, young adults (21 to 39).
- **The guideline floor.** The American College of Sports Medicine (2011 position stand) recommends flexibility exercises for each major muscle-tendon group, a total of 60 seconds per exercise, on two or more days a week. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/ Expert consensus for healthy adults; it is a maintenance floor, not the dose for gaining range.

Rule: when range of motion is the goal, hold each static stretch about 30 seconds, about 60 seconds in all per muscle per session, and stretch that muscle on as many days as the week allows (five is the target, two the floor). Don't lengthen holds past a minute for more range; add days instead.
