# Achilles tendinopathy, kneecap pain and the pain-monitoring rule

Covers three gaps in `knowledge/injury-playbook.md`: midportion Achilles tendinopathy (filed under Calf), patellofemoral (kneecap) pain, and the playbook's general pain rule (two or three out of ten at most; worse next morning means too much). It does not cover insertional Achilles pain, Achilles rupture, acute knee injuries or knee surgery.

Read this first: none of the three guidelines asked for could be opened. jospt.org and orthopt.org refused every fetch (403), and APTA's page is for members only. So every guideline claim below comes from a secondary page that was opened, and each bullet says which. In order of trust: a peer-reviewed open review (J Athl Train 2020, on PMC), then Guideline Central's summary, then a physical therapy clinic's blog. Every source was opened on 2026-11-02.

## 1. Achilles tendinopathy (midportion)

**What helps**
- Loading the calf and Achilles is the treatment with the most evidence. A peer-reviewed review (Silbernagel, Hanlon and Sprague, J Athl Train 2020) rates exercise as strength-of-recommendation A, citing the 2018 JOSPT guideline (Martin and colleagues) among others. It says any mechanical loading works, eccentric or slow concentric-and-eccentric. Higher loads with slower contractions seem to help most. Isometrics are proposed as a start, but no evidence shows them better than other exercise. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7249277/ (full text read; Figure 5 is an image and could not be read).
- The same review says exercise should be done at least twice a week (Table 4). Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7249277/
- Heavy slow resistance and eccentric training gave equally good, lasting results over 12 weeks of training, with gains kept at 52 weeks (58 patients, chronic midportion tendinopathy). Heavy slow resistance had better session attendance (92 against 78 percent). Abstract only; it gives no sets, reps or frequency. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018970/ (Beyer and colleagues, Am J Sports Med 2015)
- The 2024 revision of the guideline replaces the 2018 one (Chimenti and colleagues, JOSPT 2024; citation confirmed on PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39611662/, which shows no free full text). A clinic blog's summary of it says: tendon loading is first-line (grade A), at the highest load tolerated, at least three times a week; volume and intensity are adjusted to how irritable the tendon is; complete rest is not indicated and activity continues within pain tolerance. Secondary summary only, from a clinic blog. Source: https://physicaltherapyfirst.com/blog/2024-clnical-practice-guideline-for-midportion-achilles-tendinopathy/

**Dose in a published programme**
- The J Athl Train review's Table 2 gives a phased programme. Weeks 1 to 2, once a day: two-legged heel raises 3 x 10 to 15, one-legged 3 x 10, eccentric 3 x 10, seated 3 x 10. Weeks 2 to 5, daily: heel raises off a step edge 3 x 15, quick-rebounding raises 3 x 20. Weeks 3 to 12: heavier loads with added weight two or three times a week, plus plyometrics. Added weight starts around 10 lb (4.5 kg) and goes up when three sets of 15 are easy. Plyometrics start on two legs, then one. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7249277/
- The same review: complete rest may do harm; deep-water running and cycling can keep fitness while the tendon settles. Before running and jumping again, pain during daily walking should be minimal (1 to 2 out of 10), and pain above 2 out of 10 during 20 single-leg hops makes starting running hard. These are return-to-running gates, not an exercise ceiling. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7249277/

**The pain-monitoring model (Silbernagel 2007)**
- The 2007 trial (38 patients) found that continuing running and jumping under a pain-monitoring model, alongside a tendon-loading programme, did no harm compared with six weeks of rest from those activities; both groups improved. The abstract confirms a pain-monitoring model was used but gives no pain numbers. Abstract only. Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17307888/ and https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/18389
- The model's numbers for the 2007 trial come only from a clinic blog's review of it: pain during activity was not to reach 5 out of 10; pain after exercise could reach 5 but should be gone by the next morning; pain and stiffness were not to rise from week to week. Secondary source only; not confirmed on a primary page. Source: https://physicaltherapyfirst.com/blog/continued-sports-activity-using-a-pain-monitoring-model-during-rehabilitation-in-patients-with-achilles-tendinopathy/
- An earlier Silbernagel trial (2001, Scand J Med Sci Sports) used the same kind of rule: up to 5 out of 10, settled by the following morning, with no morning stiffness. This is reported by the Smith 2017 review (see section 3), which included the 2001 trial, not the 2007 one. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5739826/
- The J Athl Train review says to check symptoms after training, "especially the following day", before raising intensity, and to keep a daily pain log. Its Table 3 sorts activities as light (1 to 2 out of 10 during), medium (2 to 3) and high (4 to 5), with 0, 2 and 3 recovery days; this sets recovery, not a cap. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7249277/

## 2. Kneecap pain (patellofemoral pain)

- The 2019 guideline (Willy and colleagues, JOSPT 2019) was not opened. Its abstract (PEDro record) says the pain is around the front of the kneecap, often comes on gradually, and is made worse by squatting, prolonged sitting, stairs, jumping and running. Abstract only. Source: https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/58858
- Guideline Central's summary of the 2019 guideline: exercise combining hip-targeted and knee-targeted work, grade A, to cut pain and improve function in the short, medium and long term. Hip work aims at the muscles at the back and side of the hip and may be favoured early. Knee work can be weight-bearing (resisted squats) or not (resisted knee extension). Combined work is preferred over knee work alone. No dose given. Secondary summary only. Source: https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/8381/
- The same summary: education (grade F, expert opinion) may cover load management, body weight, sticking with the exercise, and fear of movement. Load management appears only there, not as its own recommendation. Prefabricated foot orthoses (grade A, up to 6 weeks, with exercise) and tailored taping (grade B, short term) may help alongside exercise; braces and sleeves should not be prescribed (grade B); manual therapy alone and dry needling should not be used (grade A). Exercise is the core of any combined treatment. Secondary summary only. Source: https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/8381/
- Agreement with the 2016 consensus the playbook cites: the consensus recommends exercise for pain in the short, medium and long term, and combining hip and knee exercise over knee alone. It gives no dose and no rule on pain during exercise. The 2019 summary agrees on hip plus knee. The check was against a summary, not the guideline itself. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4975825/ (Crossley and colleagues, BJSM 2016, full text)
- A family-medicine review (AAFP 2019) suggests strengthening and flexibility work three times a week for six to eight weeks, aimed at the hip, trunk and knee, and says aggravating movements should be avoided while the patient otherwise stays as active as possible. It rests on a Cochrane review and the 2016 consensus, not the 2019 guideline. Source: https://www.aafp.org/afp/2019/0115/p88

## 3. The general pain rule

- A systematic review and meta-analysis (Smith and colleagues, BJSM 2017; 7 trials, 385 people, chronic musculoskeletal pain) found exercise allowed into pain gave a small short-term benefit over pain-free exercise (moderate-quality evidence in the sensitivity analysis), and no clear difference in the medium or long term. Its conclusion: pain during exercise need not be a barrier to a good outcome. Full text read. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5739826/
- The thresholds its trials used: up to 5 out of 10 in two shoulder trials and in Silbernagel's Achilles trial, and 50 mm on a 100 mm scale in a low back trial; others gave no number. Pain was to settle by the next session, by the next morning, or after the session. No trial used 2 or 3 out of 10. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5739826/
- Its limits: few, small trials; no participant was blinded; no trial recorded how much pain people actually felt; and it holds shoulder, Achilles, low back and heel trials but no knee trials, so it says nothing about kneecap pain. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5739826/

What this means for the playbook's rule:
- The next-morning half is supported: worse the next morning, or pain not settled by then, means too much (Silbernagel 2001 via Smith; the J Athl Train review's "especially the following day").
- The two-or-three-out-of-ten half is stricter than any source opened. The trials allowed up to 5. Keeping 2 to 3 is house judgment, reasonable for a listener training alone with no clinician watching, and should be marked as such.

## Not confirmed

- **The 2018 Achilles guideline's own text and grades.** jospt.org and orthopt.org returned 403; a Scribd copy would not render. The "grade A mechanical loading" and "at least twice weekly within pain tolerance" wording appeared only in search snippets, and is not used here. What is used comes from the J Athl Train review, which cites the guideline.
- **The 2024 Achilles revision's text and grades.** Known only from PubMed's citation and a clinic blog. Its "three times a week, highest load tolerated" is unconfirmed on a primary page.
- **The 2007 Silbernagel trial's pain numbers** (under 5 during, up to 5 after, gone by morning, no rise week to week). Only a clinic blog states them; PubMed and PEDro give none. The 2001 trial's rule, via Smith 2017, is the better-supported version.
- **The 2019 kneecap guideline's own text, grades and any dose.** Known only from Guideline Central's summary and the PEDro abstract.
- **Any 2 or 3 out of 10 ceiling during exercise,** for musculoskeletal rehab in general or tendinopathy. None found.
- **A pain threshold for kneecap pain specifically.** None found in a source worth citing (search turned up only a podcast and a patient leaflet, not cited). Smith 2017 has no knee trials.
- **What to avoid or modify in kneecap pain beyond the general.** No source opened gives squat depth, range or stair rules.
- **Heavy slow resistance and eccentric doses** (sets, reps, frequency) from Beyer 2015: the abstract gives only 12 weeks.
- **Silbernagel and Crossley 2015** (the return-to-sport programme, JOSPT) was not opened; the J Athl Train review's Figure 5, which reprints the pain-monitoring model, is an image and could not be read.
