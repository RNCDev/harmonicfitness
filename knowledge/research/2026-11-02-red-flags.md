# Red flags research

Scope: the signs that should stop a session and send the listener to a clinician, in five groups: the heart, a clot in the leg (DVT), the low back (cauda equina and other serious causes), an Achilles rupture and the neck (artery problems and the spinal cord). It does not cover diagnosis, treatment, tests a clinician runs, or who is at risk, and it does not check the playbook's current joint signs. Every source cited here was opened in this session. Where only an abstract could be read, the entry says so. Paraphrase is mine; quotes are short.

Each source states how urgent a sign is: call 999 or go to A&E, call 111 or get an urgent GP appointment, or see a GP. The current playbook treats every sign the same. The tiers below come from the sources, and a tool could keep them apart.

## 1. The heart

- PAR-Q+ (2025 version, the PAR-Q+ Collaboration's own form): question 2 asks about "pain in your chest at rest, during your daily activities of living, OR when you do physical activity". Question 3 asks about losing balance because of dizziness, or losing consciousness, in the last 12 months. It says to answer no if the dizziness came from over-breathing, including during vigorous exercise. Page 1 also says to delay becoming more active when "your health changes", or with a temporary illness such as a cold or fever. https://eparmedx.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/PARQPlus2025Fillable.pdf
- MedlinePlus, "Being active when you have heart disease" (NIH, reviewed 2024-08-05): stop and rest if you feel dizzy or lightheaded, or have chest pain, an irregular heartbeat or pulse, shortness of breath or nausea. Call your provider for chest pain, pressure, tightness or heaviness, including in the arm, neck or jaw. Call right away if the symptoms are very bad or don't go away after you stop. The page is written for people with heart disease. https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000094.htm
- NHS, fainting: call 999 if the person "fainted while exercising". Also call 999 for fainting with chest pain or an irregular, fluttering heartbeat. https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/fainting/
- NHS, heart palpitations: call 999 or go to A&E if palpitations don't go away, or come with chest pain, shortness of breath, or feeling faint or fainting. Call 111 if those symptoms happened but have stopped. See a GP if palpitations keep coming back, last longer than a few minutes, or you have a heart condition. https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heart-palpitations/
- NHS, heart attack symptoms: chest pain that may feel crushing or squeezing and may spread to the arms, neck or jaw, with shortness of breath, nausea, sweating, or pale, blue or grey skin. Call 999 at once for tight or squeezing chest pain or severe breathing difficulty. Call 111 if breathlessness suddenly gets worse, or chest pain comes with feeling sick or a racing, slow or irregular heartbeat. Don't drive yourself. https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heart-attack/symptoms/
- Riebe et al. 2015, ACSM preparticipation screening (abstract only, PubMed 26473759): exercise-related cardiac events are often preceded by warning signs or symptoms. The new screening model rests on current activity, signs or symptoms or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and the intensity wanted. The abstract does not list the signs. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Riebe+Updating+ACSM%27s+recommendations+for+exercise+preparticipation+health+screening
- Thompson et al. 2007, AHA scientific statement (abstract only, PubMed 17468391): vigorous activity briefly raises the risk of sudden cardiac death and heart attack in susceptible people, most in the least active. Among strategies it calls prudent but untested is "promptly evaluating possible prodromal symptoms". The abstract does not list them. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17468391/
- Franklin et al. 2020, AHA scientific statement (abstract only, PubMed 32100573): repeats that vigorous activity, mostly in unfit people, can briefly raise those risks. The abstract says nothing about warning symptoms. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32100573/

## 2. A clot in the leg (DVT)

- NHS, DVT: throbbing pain in one leg (rarely both), usually the calf or thigh; swelling in one leg; red, blue or darkened skin around the painful area; swollen veins. Ask for an urgent GP appointment or call 111 if you think you have DVT. Call 999 or go to A&E with those symptoms and shortness of breath or chest pain. Situations that raise the risk include recent surgery, a journey of more than four hours, three days or more in bed, and a recent hospital stay. https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/deep-vein-thrombosis-dvt/
- The NHS page does not mention warmth. MedlinePlus (NIH, updated 2025-11-20) lists warmth and tenderness over the vein, pain or swelling, and skin redness. It names pulmonary embolism as the danger but lists no signs of it. https://medlineplus.gov/deepveinthrombosis.html

## 3. The low back

- GIRFT National Suspected Cauda Equina Syndrome Pathway (February 2023, page 6), co-badged with the British Association of Spine Surgeons, the British Orthopaedic Association, the Chartered Society of Physiotherapy, the Royal College of Radiologists and others (page 3): back or leg pain with any of these, started in the last two weeks, warrants emergency referral to a hospital with emergency MRI, the scan "as soon as possible, and certainly within four hours" of the request: difficulty starting to pee or a changed feeling of the flow; altered feeling around the anus, the perineum or the genitals (from a small patch to the area of a saddle); severe or worsening weakness in both legs; loss of the feeling that the rectum is full; and sexual dysfunction. CES "does not have a set clinical pattern", and a normal examination doesn't rule it out. https://spinal.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/National-Suspected-Cauda-Equina-Pathway-February-2023-FINAL-V1-1.pdf
- The same pathway gives a warning sign: sciatica that starts suddenly in both legs, or spreads from one leg to both, may come before CES. Without the symptoms above it needs an urgent referral, within two weeks, rather than an emergency one.
- The same pathway quotes the patient-facing warning card of the Musculoskeletal Association of Chartered Physiotherapists. With pain down one or both legs and/or severe low back pain, in any combination, seek help at once: numbness or pins and needles between the inner thighs or genitals; numbness around the back passage or buttocks; feeling different when wiping; more difficulty starting or stopping a pee; less feeling when peeing; leaking or a new need for pads; not knowing whether the bladder is full or empty; being unable to stop a bowel movement, or leaking; less feeling when passing a stool; changes in erection or ejaculation; loss of feeling in the genitals during sex.
- NHS, back pain: call 999 or go to A&E for pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs; loss of feeling around the genitals or anus; changes in bladder or bowels; changes in sexual feeling or function; chest pain; or back pain that started after a serious accident. Ask for an urgent GP appointment or call 111 if you feel hot, cold, shivery or generally unwell, or the pain is severe and sudden or getting worse quickly. See a GP if you have lost weight without trying, have a lump or a change in your back's shape, have pain that doesn't ease with rest or is worse at night, have pain worse when sneezing, coughing or on the toilet, have pain between the shoulder blades, or haven't improved after a few weeks. https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/

## 4. An Achilles rupture

- OrthoInfo (AAOS's patient site): you may hear a pop; severe pain and swelling near the heel can follow; you may be unable to stand on the toes of the injured leg or walk on it. A doctor may squeeze the calf to see whether the foot moves. The page gives no review date. https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/achilles-tendon-rupture-tear/
- St George's Hospital emergency department leaflet (review date May 2026, now past): people often say it felt as if someone had hit them on the back of the heel, and they turned round to find no one there. It can come with a snapping or popping sound. Afterwards walking or standing on tiptoe is usually difficult, sometimes with swelling or bruising. https://www.stgeorges.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/AAE_ATR.pdf
- Oxford University Hospitals emergency department leaflet (October 2025): immediate sharp pain at the back of the ankle; being unable to rise onto the toes or push off from the foot; difficulty walking. https://www.ouh.nhs.uk/media/l1lhs3h3/116457achilles.pdf
- NHS, tendonitis: a sudden, severe pain during an activity, sometimes with a pop or snap, may be a ruptured tendon. Get help from 111. https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tendonitis/

## 5. The neck

- IFOMPT Cervical Framework, Rushton et al. (the authors' manuscript in a university repository, CC BY 4.0; the repository lists JOSPT 2022 and doesn't say which version it is). It covers vascular pathologies of the neck, its term for what used to be called cervical arterial dysfunction. https://hsu.repository.guildhe.ac.uk/id/eprint/188/1/International%20Framework%20for%20Examination%20of%20the%20Cervical%20Region%20for%20Potential.pdf
  - Features reported with dissection (Table 4): headache (81%), neck pain, visual disturbance, pins and needles in the arm, face or leg, and dizziness.
  - Features of a vertebrobasilar dissection (Table 6), most common first: unsteadiness or poor coordination; slurred speech or trouble finding words; leg or arm weakness; difficulty swallowing; nausea or vomiting; a drooping face; dizziness; a drooping eyelid; loss of consciousness; confusion; drowsiness.
  - Carotid dissection (Table 7) most often shows a drooping eyelid, arm weakness and a drooping face.
  - Suspicion should be high when sudden neck or head pain is described as "unlike any other".
  - Signs to watch for while taking the history: a changed gait, subtle unsteadiness, upper motor neuron signs, cranial nerve problems, and behaviour suggesting upper neck instability, such as anxiety or holding up the head.
  - Action: refer for immediate medical investigation when vascular disease is suspected. In one worked case, unsteadiness and vomiting after neck treatment called for an ambulance.
  - Table 1 also names a sudden severe headache with a stiff neck, visual disturbance, light sensitivity, slurred speech, sickness or one-sided weakness (subarachnoid haemorrhage), and "thunderclap" headaches.
- Leeds Community Healthcare NHS Trust, degenerative cervical myelopathy (spinal cord compression in the neck): tingling or numbness in the arms, hands, legs or feet; clumsy hands; trouble with fine tasks such as buttons, zips or handwriting; weak or heavy limbs, dropping things or tripping; poor balance or falls; difficulty walking; worsening arm or leg pain; recent changes in bladder function. Seek medical attention for new or worsening symptoms. The page gives no urgency tier. https://leedscommunityhealthcare.nhs.uk/our-services-a-z/musculoskeletal-msk/neck-problems/known-and-diagnosed-neck-problems/degenerative-cervical-myelopathy/
- NHS, neck pain: only see-a-GP items. "Other symptoms, like pins and needles or a cold arm", could be something more serious. The page has no 111 or 999 items. https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/neck-pain/

## Not confirmed

- **NICE NG59 Recommendations page** (https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/Recommendations): 403 on both tries. What it says about alternative diagnoses and red flags is unchecked, so the playbook's existing citation of it went unverified this session.
- **NICE CKS sciatica and low back pain pages:** 403. A 2018 change to the CKS cauda equina red flags (adding sciatica in both legs and loss of feeling in the genitals) came only from search summaries and legal commentary, which aren't cited here. The GIRFT pathway covers the same ground.
- **NICE NG158** (venous thromboembolic diseases): 403. The DVT signs here come from the NHS and MedlinePlus only. Warmth comes from MedlinePlus alone; the NHS page doesn't list it.
- **Riebe et al. 2015, full text:** open copies were refused as unofficial, the publisher wants payment, and only the abstract was read. ACSM's list of signs and symptoms (chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or fainting, palpitations, ankle swelling, calf pain on walking, a known murmur, unusual fatigue) was seen only on flashcard and study-guide sites and is not cited. "Breathlessness out of proportion to the effort" therefore has no ACSM source here. MedlinePlus (shortness of breath) and NHS (worsening breathlessness) are the nearest.
- **AHA statements, full text** (Thompson 2007, Franklin 2020): ahajournals.org returned 403 and only the abstracts were read. Which warning symptoms came before exercise deaths, and how often, is unconfirmed: the percentages came from a blog in a search summary.
- **heart.org, warning signs of a heart attack:** 403. The NHS heart attack page stands in.
- **AAOS clinical practice guideline on acute Achilles tendon rupture (2009/2010), full text:** not opened. PubMed's abstract (20675643) names no exam findings. The guideline's diagnostic rule (two or more of a positive calf-squeeze test, weaker push-down, a palpable gap and more ankle give) came only from search summaries.
- **A palpable gap as a listener's sign:** no opened source lists it. The "kick" wording is confirmed only as "as if someone had hit them on the back of the heel", in one hospital leaflet whose review date has passed.
- **NHS pages for cauda equina syndrome and Achilles rupture:** the addresses tried (`/conditions/cauda-equina-syndrome/`, `/conditions/achilles-tendon-rupture/`, `/conditions/achilles-tendinopathy/`) returned 404.
- **Blanpied et al. 2017, JOSPT neck pain guideline:** jospt.org and the orthopt.org PDF returned 403. The Europe PMC abstract (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:28666405&resultType=core&format=json) mentions no red flags, referral, myelopathy or artery signs, so nothing from it is used.
- **IFOMPT framework, official PDF on ifompt.org** (2020 and 2023 addendum): 404. The repository manuscript was read instead. The published citation (JOSPT 2023;53(1):7-22) was seen only in a search snippet.
- **"5 Ds and 3 Ns", drop attacks and double vision:** the IFOMPT text doesn't use the mnemonic or say "drop attack". Double vision (diplopia) appears only for giant cell arteritis, not for dissection, where it says "visual disturbance". These three aren't confirmed as neck artery signs.
- **How urgent the spinal cord signs are:** the myelopathy page says only "seek medical attention". No opened source says whether new hand clumsiness or unsteadiness means the same day or a GP visit.
- **Exercise-specific DVT advice** (for example, calf pain that is a clot rather than a strain): no opened source tells them apart.
