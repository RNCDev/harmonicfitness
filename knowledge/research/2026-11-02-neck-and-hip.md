# Neck and hip: what guidelines say about exercise around a known problem

Covers what clinical practice guidelines and one trial say about exercising with an assessed neck or hip problem: which exercise helps, what to keep light, the signs to stop or refer, and whether pain during exercise is acceptable. It doesn't cover acute injuries (a fresh strain, a fall, a dislocation), surgery or rehab after it, children, or manual therapy, needling and other clinic-only treatments. Every source below was opened on 2026-11-02; where only an abstract or a copy of a paper was read, it says so.

## Neck

**Source read:** Blanpied and colleagues, "Neck Pain: Revision 2017", J Orthop Sports Phys Ther 2017;47(7):A1-A83, doi 10.2519/jospt.2017.0302. The journal's page (jospt.org) refused the fetch; the full English PDF was read from a university course copy: https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/318008/mod_resource/content/1/revisione%202017.pdf. Its recommendations are graded A to F. The orthopt.org PDF of this guideline is a Chinese translation and wasn't used.

**Stages.** The guideline sorts neck pain into four kinds (with mobility deficits; with movement coordination problems, including whiplash; with headache; with pain radiating into the arm) and three stages: acute under 6 weeks, subacute 6 to 12 weeks, chronic over 12 weeks. It ties the stages to irritability: acute usually means pain at rest or early in a movement, subacute pain in mid-range that worsens at end range, chronic pain only with sustained end-range positions. It says how the problem behaves matters more than how long it has lasted. (Component 3 of the figure, page A23.)

**What helps (from the Summary of Recommendations, pages A2 and A3).**
- Neck pain with mobility deficits, acute (grade B): neck range-of-motion exercises, and shoulder-blade and arm strengthening "to enhance program adherence" (plus thoracic manipulation, a clinic treatment).
- Same, subacute (grade B): neck and shoulder girdle endurance exercises.
- Same, chronic (grade B): mixed exercise for the neck and shoulder-blade region (coordination, proprioception and postural training, stretching, strengthening, endurance, aerobic conditioning), alongside clinic treatments. Grade C: neck, shoulder girdle and trunk endurance work, and advice that promotes an active lifestyle.
- Whiplash-type neck pain, acute (grade B): return to normal activities that don't provoke it as soon as possible, use a collar as little as possible, do postural and mobility exercises; reassurance that recovery is expected within 2 to 3 months.
- Whiplash-type, chronic (grade C): an individualized, progressive submaximal exercise programme (strengthening, endurance, flexibility, coordination).
- Neck pain with headache, chronic (grade B): neck and shoulder girdle stretching, strengthening and endurance exercise, with manual therapy.
- Neck pain radiating into the arm, chronic (grade B): education and counseling to encourage taking part in work and exercise.
- The treatment figure (page A23) lists, for acute mobility deficits, "advice to stay active plus home cervical ROM and isometric exercise", and, for acute whiplash-type pain with a quick recovery expected, "home exercise: pain-free cervical ROM and postural element" and "monitor for acceptable progress". This is the guideline's proposed model, not a graded recommendation.

**Dose.** The guideline says trial doses (intensity, duration, frequency) are reported too variably to translate confidently into practice, and suggests combining trial doses with clinical judgment (page A25). One included result: a 1-year home programme of neck flexion endurance exercise 3 times a week, plus arm strengthening and stretching, beat aerobic exercise for pain and function in chronic neck pain (page A29 evidence table). That is one trial's dose, not a recommendation.

**Harms.** Adverse events were rarely reported; for exercise or manual therapy the only consistently reported problem was "a mild transient exacerbation of symptoms" (page A25).

**Stop and refer signs.**
- Grade A: screen for serious pathology (infection, cancer, cardiac involvement, arterial insufficiency, upper cervical ligament insufficiency, unexplained cranial nerve dysfunction, fracture) and refer as needed (pages A2 and A15).
- Radiating pain: pain into the arm with motor, reflex or sensory changes (pins and needles, numbness) points to a nerve root problem (page A15).
- Cord compression (myelopathy) can bring signs in the arms or legs and bowel or bladder problems (page A15).
- Headache that worsens with exertion may need referral for neuroimaging (page A15).
- Whiplash-type pain can come with dizziness, nausea, concentration or memory problems and other concussion-like signs (page A22).

**Second source, abstract only:** Gross and colleagues, "Exercises for mechanical neck disorders", Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2015, 27 trials. Moderate-quality evidence that strengthening of the neck, shoulder-blade region and arms reduces pain in chronic neck pain right after treatment and in the short term; strengthening plus stretching helps pain and function; stretching alone may not help; no evidence found for acute neck pain alone; no high-quality evidence overall; the authors call for research on optimal dose. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25629215/

## Hip

The sources cover three different hip problems, and their advice differs. None covers a fresh strain, a bruise or a fall.

### Hip osteoarthritis

**Source read (current):** Koc and colleagues, "Hip Pain and Mobility Deficits—Hip Osteoarthritis: Revision 2025", J Orthop Sports Phys Ther 2025;55(11):CPG1-CPG31, doi 10.2519/jospt.2025.0301. Full PDF: https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/Hip_pain_and_mobility_deficits_hip_osteoarthritis_2025.pdf. The orthopt.org copy read starts at the examination section; its Summary of Recommendations pages weren't in it.

- Exercise (grade A): an individualized exercise programme, possibly including water-based exercise, to improve motion, strength, function and pain, 1 to 5 times a week, 30 to 120 minutes each, over 5 to 16 weeks (page CPG14).
- In the evidence behind it, progressive strength training did better than Nordic walking, which did better than unsupervised home exercise, for function at 2, 4 and 12 months (Bieler and colleagues, page CPG12). A home programme without supervision was the weakest of the three.
- Adding exercise to education beats education alone (page CPG14).
- One trial found no difference between fast and slow resistance exercise over 8 weeks; its programme was hip abduction lying on the back, hip extension lying face down, and seated hip flexion and knee extension, 2 sets of 10 for 2 weeks, then 3 sets of 10 (page CPG14).
- Harms: one systematic review found few adverse events and suggested exercise-related risk of harm is minimal (page CPG12).
- Education (grade B): activity modification, exercise, weight reduction when overweight, and ways of unloading the arthritic joint (page CPG16).
- Functional, gait and balance training (grade C, unchanged because no new studies): give it when activity, balance or walking problems are observed (page CPG17).

**Older version, for what 2025 doesn't restate:** Cibulka and colleagues, "Hip Pain and Mobility Deficits—Hip Osteoarthritis: Revision 2017", J Orthop Sports Phys Ther 2017;47(6):A1-A37, doi 10.2519/jospt.2017.0301. Full PDF: https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/Hip%20OA%20Revision%202017.pdf
- Grade F: revise the diagnosis or refer when the symptoms don't fit the guideline's picture of hip osteoarthritis, or when they aren't diminishing with treatment (page A2).
- Grade A: measure balance and fall risk; recommended tests include the timed single-leg stance test (page A2).
- Grade A (2017): individualized flexibility, strengthening and endurance exercise for hip range of motion and specific muscle weakness, 1 to 5 times a week over 6 to 12 weeks (page A3).

### Hip joint pain without arthritis (impingement, labral tears)

**Source read:** Enseki and colleagues, "Hip Pain and Movement Dysfunction Associated With Nonarthritic Hip Joint Pain: Revision 2023", J Orthop Sports Phys Ther 2023;53(7):CPG1-CPG70, doi 10.2519/jospt.2023.0302. Full PDF: https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/jospt.2023.0302.pdf

- Multimodal care (grade B): activity modification plus strengthening of specific hip muscles (iliopsoas, gluteus medius, gluteus maximus, hip internal and external rotators), the trunk (abdominals and back extensors) and the legs in general (lunges, squats, step-ups), with stretching, balance work, movement correction and manual therapy, particularly for impingement and labral injuries (page CPG24).
- In the studies behind it, activity modification meant a period of rest from sport and avoiding pain-provoking positions or movements; "decreasing the amount of hip flexion" was often part of it (page CPG23).
- Education (grade C): modify aggravating factors. One retrospective case series suggested limiting squatting, leg crossing, pivoting, excessive activity and sitting on the floor, often painful with impingement (page CPG26).
- Exercise (grade C): therapeutic exercise for the mobility, flexibility and strength deficits found on examination. Limited evidence of benefit within 6 to 8 weeks; weight-bearing strengthening should keep the hip and pelvis controlled; consensus statements recommend physiotherapist-led exercise for up to 3 months for impingement (page CPG26).
- Dose: the guideline says the studies lack detail on exercises, dose and criteria for progression, and there's no consensus on the best management (page CPG24).
- Refer: the guideline lists conditions that can mimic hip joint pain, for which a physiotherapist should promptly refer when appropriate, including stress fracture of the femur or pelvis, osteonecrosis of the femoral head, septic arthritis, tumour, infection of the bone, low back problems, hernia and nerve entrapment (page CPG15).

**Warwick Agreement, abstract only:** an international consensus on femoroacetabular impingement syndrome; it says diagnosis needs symptoms, clinical signs and imaging together, and that suitable treatments are conservative care, rehabilitation, and arthroscopic or open surgery. The abstract names no exercises or doses. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27629403/

### Pain on the outside of the hip (gluteal tendinopathy)

**Source read:** Mellor and colleagues, "Education plus exercise versus corticosteroid injection use versus a wait and see approach on global outcome and pain from gluteal tendinopathy", BMJ 2018;361:k1662 (the LEAP trial). Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5930290/; abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29720374/

- 204 people aged 35 to 70 with outer hip pain for over three months, at least 4 out of 10, confirmed by examination and MRI.
- Education plus exercise: 14 physiotherapist sessions over 8 weeks, plus 4 to 6 exercises daily at home: strengthening the hip abductors and controlling the hip from drifting inward (adduction) in daily movement. Education covered tendon care, especially how much load and how to build it gradually; the discussion says it aimed to avoid movements and positions that compress the gluteal tendons against the bone at the side of the hip.
- Rated moderately better or more: 51 of 66 with education plus exercise at 8 weeks, against 38 of 65 with an injection and 20 of 68 with wait and see; at a year, 51 of 65, 36 of 63 and 31 of 60.
- The paper gives no pain limit during exercise and no next-day rule. The exercise list and its progression are in a supplement that wasn't read.

## Not confirmed

- **Pain during exercise:** no source read gives an acceptable pain score during or after exercise, or a next-day rule, for the neck or the hip. The neck guideline's model says home range-of-motion work for acute whiplash-type pain should be "pain-free"; nothing else states a level. The playbook's two-or-three-out-of-ten ceiling stays house judgment.
- **Loosening gates:** no source sets pass marks for moving on. Not for the neck (slow head turns, overhead work), nor the hip (no night pain or limp, a pain-free loaded squat and hinge). The hip osteoarthritis guideline names the timed single-leg stance test as a measure but gives no pass mark.
- **Overhead pressing with a neck problem:** nothing found on whether to avoid or allow it. The playbook's rule that overhead work clears a delicate neck is house judgment.
- **Neck home dose:** none recommended; the guideline says trial doses don't translate. The 3-times-a-week endurance programme is one trial.
- **Hip dose at home:** the 2025 range (1 to 5 times a week, 30 to 120 minutes, 5 to 16 weeks) comes from mostly supervised programmes; unsupervised home exercise did worst in the trials it cites.
- **Which positions to avoid for gluteal tendinopathy:** not in the LEAP main text read (the specific positions are in a supplement and education material that weren't opened).
- **Warwick Agreement full text:** only the abstract was read (BJSM refused the fetch, and it isn't in PubMed Central). Its exercise aims weren't verified. A Dutch sports medicine society's summary was seen but isn't the paper and isn't used.
- **Cochrane 2015 neck review:** abstract only; its effect sizes aren't used.
- **Publisher pages:** jospt.org refused the fetch for the neck guideline, and bjsm.bmj.com for the Warwick Agreement; the copies named above were read instead. The 2025 hip osteoarthritis copy lacked the Summary of Recommendations pages.
- **Acute hip strains, bruises, hip flexor strains, bursitis without tendinopathy, hip replacement:** not covered by any source here.
- **Neck:** nothing found specific to a disc injury in a fit adult, to neck pain from training, or to which catalog movements (carries, shrugs, presses) load the neck.
