# Elbow, wrist and rib: what the guidelines say about training around them

Covers three areas the injury playbook had no source for: lateral elbow pain (tennis elbow), rehab after a broken wrist (distal radius fracture) and the scaphoid fracture that can pass for a sprain, and rib pain (rib fracture, costochondritis, rib stress injury). It doesn't cover medial elbow pain, de Quervain's, wrist sprain rehab or intercostal muscle strain: searched for, not opened or not found (see Not confirmed). Each finding says how much of the source was opened: "full text opened" means the full text was opened and the relevant sections read.

## Elbow (lateral elbow tendinopathy)

**Full text opened:** the JOSPT clinical practice guideline, Lucado and colleagues 2022, from the orthopt.org copy (https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/jospt.2022.0302.pdf; PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36453071/). It reviews studies published up to November 2021.

- Exercise is the main recommendation. Clinicians "should use isometric, concentric, and/or eccentric" resisted exercise of the wrist extensors for subacute or chronic lateral elbow tendinopathy (grade B). Also grade B: wrist-extension strengthening combined with other treatment such as manual therapy. Grade C: add shoulder and shoulder-blade muscle work when impairments are found. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG2 and CPG27–28.
- Dose: the guideline suggests 3 sets of 15 reps for 6 to 12 weeks. It takes that from a systematic review and calls it "best available evidence and expert opinion". It also says no study described how the load was chosen or progressed. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG26.
- The pain rule: loads "should not exacerbate symptoms". Progress from isometric to isotonic to eccentric "as tolerated without exacerbation of symptoms". The guideline sets no pain score. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG26.
- An algorithm the guideline describes starts with pain-free contractions at low resistance, about 20 to 40 percent of a maximal contraction. It moves above 40 percent only in a later phase, then lengthens levers and adds weight through the arm. This is expert opinion (evidence level V). The phased approach that comes from it is a grade F recommendation, aimed at people with demanding jobs, hobbies or sport. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG26.
- Harms: "No adverse effects of any type of exercise were reported" in the studies reviewed. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG26.
- Wrist-extensor stretching alone seems to have no long-term effect compared with stretching plus strengthening. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG26.
- Course: the guideline says the condition often lasts. More than half of patients seen in general medical care still have symptoms after a year, and up to 20 percent report pain 3 to 5 years after care. Protection from repetitive irritation may help limit flare-ups and recurrence. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG10.
- Self-reported nerve symptoms predicted more pain and disability at 8 weeks in one prognosis study. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG11.
- Things that mimic it and need referral include cervical radiculopathy (a pinched nerve in the neck) and radial tunnel syndrome. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG13.
- Bracing: a forearm strap or wrist brace worn during activity may give immediate relief (grade F, expert opinion). The evidence conflicts for longer-term relief (grade D). Source: the guideline, pp. CPG33.

**Trials, abstract only:**
- Unsupervised daily isometric exercise for 8 weeks, after one session with a physiotherapist, beat wait-and-see. It lowered the tennis-elbow disability score and worst pain. It didn't improve the global rating of change or pain-free grip. 40 people with symptoms for 6 weeks or more. The authors doubt it works as the only treatment. The abstract doesn't give the dose or the pain allowed. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31425384/ (Vuvan and colleagues, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2020).
- In 165 people with tennis elbow for more than 6 weeks, eight weeks of physiotherapy plus a placebo injection beat the placebo injection alone at 4 weeks: 39 against 10 percent completely recovered or much better. There was no difference at 26 weeks or a year. A steroid injection did worse than placebo at a year, with more recurrence (54 against 12 percent). The abstract describes the physiotherapy only as "multimodal". Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23385272/ (Coombes and colleagues, JAMA 2013).

## Wrist (distal radius fracture, and the scaphoid)

These findings are about clinician-directed rehab after a broken wrist. They don't apply to a sprain, tendon pain or a wrist that's simply sore.

**Full text opened:** the JOSPT clinical practice guideline on distal radius fracture rehabilitation, Mehta and colleagues 2024, from the orthopt.org copy (https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/mehta_et_al_2024_distal_radius_fracture_rehabilitation.pdf).

- Early movement: after surgery, start hand, wrist, elbow and shoulder active range of motion, with light daily activity, within the first 3 weeks (grade A). Source: the guideline, pp. CPG2.
- Early gripping: start light progressive strengthening at 2 weeks after surgery, or in the second week of a cast (grade B). Examples are squeezing a towel or putty and light-load gripping. In a cast, this applies only to an uncomplicated, stable fracture with good alignment and no pain on the little-finger side of the wrist. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG2.
- Therapeutic exercise should be timed to the type of fracture treatment and how stable the fracture is (grade B). It includes progressive resistance work for both arms that covers the shoulder and shoulder-blade muscles. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG3.
- Phases: a cast usually stays on for 4 to 6 weeks. Wrist movement starts once the surgeon confirms the fracture has healed enough. The strengthening phase "typically starts around 6 to 8 weeks" and "requires adequate fracture healing and stability". It works up to power gripping and loading through the whole arm. Take care with pain on the little-finger side, a bone that healed out of line, or positive ulnar variance. Timelines vary from person to person. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG11–13.
- Supervision: people aged 60 or over, or those with complications, should attend therapy at least weekly plus home exercise (grade B). A therapist should be the one who teaches the home program (grade A). For younger people without complications, the evidence conflicts (grade D). Source: the guideline, pp. CPG2.
- Warning signs: "extreme digital stiffness, hand hypersensitivity, and/or trophic changes in the skin" call for an urgent therapy consult. Type 1 complex regional pain syndrome follows 1 to 32 percent of these fractures in the studies cited. High early pain predicts persistent pain. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG2, CPG9 and CPG12.
- The guideline says nothing about bearing weight through the hand (push-ups, planks, all fours). It gives no criteria for returning to sport: X-rays confirming the bone has healed help guide how hard exercise can be and when to return to work and contact sport, which is the treating team's decision. Source: the guideline, pp. CPG11.

**Summary article, full text opened:** the AAOS and ASSH 2020 guideline, as summarised by Kamal and Shapiro in 2022. It found no difference between a home exercise program and supervised therapy, with inconsistent evidence (strength: limited). Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9196973

**Scaphoid, abstract only:** this review pooled 75 studies of adults seen in the emergency department with a suspected scaphoid fracture after a fall and a normal first X-ray. About 25 percent had a fracture (range 12 to 57 percent). History and examination can't rule a fracture in or out. The exception is no tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox, the hollow at the base of the thumb, which lowers the odds. MRI is the most accurate test. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24673666/ (Carpenter and colleagues, Acad Emerg Med 2014).

## Rib

No guideline was found on exercise after rib injury. What exists covers hospital care, a hospital leaflet, a chest-pain review and athlete reports.

- **Rib fracture, hospital care (full text opened):** a systematic review of 16 studies (2,034 adults) found that physiotherapy, including the active cycle of breathing technique, was linked to less pain and fewer lung complications. Certainty was low to very low. No study measured physical function objectively, and none looked at exercise after discharge. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9257723 (Weinberg and colleagues, S Afr J Physiother 2022).
- **Rib fracture, hospital patient leaflet (full text opened; a leaflet, not a guideline):** do breathing exercises at least 4 times a day, ideally sitting, and keep moving "little and often". Don't lift, push or pull anything that makes the pain worse. Ribs take about 4 to 6 weeks to heal, and some discomfort may last longer. Limit heavy lifting for at least 4 weeks and contact sport for at least 6. Seek help for breathlessness that slowly worsens, fever or discoloured phlegm. Call emergency services for sudden worse breathlessness, worsening chest pain, new severe pain in the tummy or shoulder, or coughing up blood. Source: https://www.worcsacute.nhs.uk/leaflets/physiotherapy-advice-and-exercises-following-rib-fractures/ (Worcestershire Acute Hospitals NHS Trust).
- **Costochondritis (full text opened):** this condition, inflamed cartilage where the ribs meet the breastbone, is a diagnosis made only after other causes of chest pain are ruled out. The pain is worse with deep breaths, coughing or stretching. The review advises resting from activities that bring on the pain, or doing them less hard, especially strenuous upper-body work such as rowing. In one study a stretching program led by a physical therapist gave a small drop in pain; that study was in people who'd had symptoms for over a year. Every treatment recommendation is grade C: expert opinion, with no high-quality trials. One observational study the review cites found pain gone in about 91 percent after 3 weeks of rest and anti-inflammatories. Chest-wall tenderness doesn't rule out a heart attack: in one emergency-department study, 12 percent of people with chest pain and chest-wall tenderness were having one. Source: https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2021/0700/p73.html (Mott and colleagues, Am Fam Physician 2021).
- **Rib stress fracture in rowers (a one-page conference abstract, opened):** no proven therapy shortens healing. Most reported cases needed about 6 to 8 weeks of rest and rehab before a full return, and the return should be gradual and guided by symptoms. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4535362/ (Vinther, BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 2015).
- A 2025 narrative review of rib stress injuries covers return to sport, but its abstract gives no phases, criteria or pain rules, and the full text wasn't read. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40472154/

## Not confirmed

- **A pain score for any of the three areas.** The elbow guideline says "should not exacerbate symptoms" and describes pain-free contractions early on. The wrist guideline says to progress "based on individual tolerance". The rib sources say to avoid what makes the pain worse. None endorses the playbook's "two or three out of ten", which stays house judgment.
- **Pass marks for loosening a limit.** No source gives grip strength matching the other side, a full cup lifted without pain, a loose fist or pain-free deep breaths as tests for moving on. The playbook's loosening signs stay house judgment.
- **Medial elbow pain (golfer's elbow).** One search found a 2013 BJSM review and a later review of eccentric exercise, but neither was opened. The search summary said the trials were mostly of lateral elbow pain. Not used.
- **Dose for isometric elbow exercise.** The Vuvan abstract gives no hold times, sets or load. The full text wasn't opened.
- **What the physiotherapy was in the Coombes trial.** The abstract says only "multimodal". The full text wasn't opened.
- **The page on lateral elbow pain in NICE's Clinical Knowledge Summaries (CKS).** It returned 403 and wasn't opened.
- **Weight bearing through the wrist** (push-ups, planks, all fours, straight-wrist push-ups on handles) after a fracture or any wrist problem. The distal radius guideline doesn't address it. The playbook's "delicate allows straight-wrist push-ups on handles" is house judgment.
- **Wrist sprain and ligament injury rehab** (including injuries of the triangular cartilage on the little-finger side). Not searched beyond the scaphoid.
- **De Quervain's tenosynovitis.** Search snippets pointed to reviews favouring a splint and injection, with little evidence for exercise. The one review tried (PMID 32301915) wouldn't open on PubMed or Europe PMC. Not used.
- **Criteria for returning to sport after a distal radius fracture.** The guideline names it as a decision X-rays help guide, without criteria.
- **Intercostal muscle strain.** No guideline or review on it was found. Search turned up only general muscle-strain reviews and clinic pages, none opened.
- **Exercise after a rib fracture once home.** The one systematic review covers only hospital care. The leaflet's 4- and 6-week figures are one NHS trust's advice.
- **The costochondritis page in NICE CKS.** It returned 403 and wasn't opened. Patient.info's summary appeared only in search results and isn't used.
- **The rib stress fracture figures** of 4 to 6 weeks off training (a 2017 systematic review) and 3 to 8 weeks to return to sport (a 2026 clinical guide). Both appeared only in search snippets and were never opened. The Aspetar journal article on rib stress injuries in rowers returned 404. Not used.
- **How the elbow and wrist guidelines were read.** The orthopt.org PDFs were read after downloading them, since WebFetch got 403. jospt.org also returned 403.
