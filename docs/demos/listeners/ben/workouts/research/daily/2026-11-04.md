## Cooking / myth_or_fact: Searing does not seal in the juices

Hook: Ben, you can keep one of the oldest kitchen rules and drop the reason people give for it.

- The claim, as said aloud. Sear a steak hard in a hot pan, and the crust seals the juices inside. It sounds sensible, and nearly every home cook has heard it. Cookbooks have repeated it for well over a century. Some trace it back to Aristotle, and the chemist Justus von Liebig pushed it again around 1850.
- The answer is no. A seared crust is not waterproof. Tests from the 1930s found that seared roasts lost as much moisture as unseared ones, or even more. Harold McGee, the food science writer, says the same: searing costs you more moisture than cooking to the same inside temperature without it.
- So why sear at all? Flavor. Heat makes the surface go brown, and amino acids and sugars react into savory taste and smell. For a brown crust the surface has to pass about 150 degrees Celsius, which is 300 Fahrenheit. Sear for the taste, never for the juice.

## Ancient Rome / explainer: The concrete that heals its own cracks

Hook: Ben, the white lumps in Roman concrete looked like mistakes for centuries, and they turned out to be the secret.

- Romans used concrete widely from about 150 BC. Some of it still stands, and the Pantheon's dome is the oldest and largest dome of unreinforced concrete in the world. For years the credit went to volcanic ash from near Naples, called pozzolana, which was shipped right across the empire.
- In 2023 a team from MIT, including Admir Masic and Linda Seymour, reported in the journal Science Advances on samples from Priverno, Italy. The odd white chunks, called lime clasts, had been blamed on sloppy mixing. The Romans likely used quicklime and mixed it hot, rather than soaking the lime first.
- Here is the test. The team cracked hot-mixed concrete on purpose and ran water through the cracks. Water reached the lime chunks, which made a calcium-rich liquid that hardened into new stone and filled the gap. Within two weeks the cracks healed and the water stopped. A copy made without quicklime never healed.

## Cooking / explainer: Pat it dry and it browns

Hook: Ben, the cheapest tool in any kitchen is a sheet of paper towel, and here is the reason it works.

- Browned food tastes better because of a set of reactions between amino acids and certain sugars, called the Maillard reaction. It makes the savory smells and flavors we love. A browned crust also gives a contrast in taste and texture with the soft inside, which makes the food more interesting to eat.
- The trick is the temperature. A brown or black crust needs the surface to go above about 150 degrees Celsius, which is 300 Fahrenheit. Water boils at only about 100 degrees, so while the surface is wet it cannot get hotter than that. The water has to be gone first.
- So pat the meat dry with a paper towel before it meets the pan. Then the heat goes into browning, not into boiling off water. Caramel is a different thing: that is only sugar. Browning meat is the Maillard reaction.
