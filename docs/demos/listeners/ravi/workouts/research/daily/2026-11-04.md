## The history of maps / serial: The Island That Was Never There

Hook: Ravi, you are about to follow a stretch of empty park path, and this is the story of a stretch of empty ocean that every map swore was land for 136 years.

Beat 1 (the report): In 1876 a whaling ship called the Velocity sailed the Coral Sea, between Australia and New Caledonia, and reported an island: Sandy Island. Nobody knows what the crew really saw. But the report went into the record, and once a place is written down, it is very hard to write it back out.

Beat 2 (the copying): The island spread the way gossip does. An Australian sea directory listed it in 1879 (single source), a German map drew it in 1881 (single source), and by 1908 a British chart showed it too. Mapmakers copied mapmakers, and no one checked. Then chartmakers began printing two small letters beside it: E D, existence doubtful.

Beat 3 (the ghost comes back): By the 1970s some official charts had dropped it; French hydrographers removed it in 1974 after a flight over the area, and Australia's followed in 1985 (single source for both dates). The ghost should have died. Instead, when the American military's mapping agency turned paper charts into a digital coastline database, the old error was carried across, and other datasets copied it.

Beat 4 (the ship): Sandy Island turned up as a black patch on Google Earth. In October 2012 the research ship Southern Surveyor sailed for the Coral Sea, led by chief scientist Maria Seton of the University of Sydney. They were studying plate tectonics, not hunting islands. But the ship's own charts showed more than a thousand metres of water exactly where the world's maps showed land. Something had to be wrong.

Beat 5 (the turn): On 22 November 2012 the ship reached the spot. No island, no reef, no sandbar: open ocean, never shallower than about 1,300 metres, roughly 4,300 feet. Within days the maps gave in. Google Maps removed the island on 26 November, and the National Geographic Society dropped it three days later. A place that had existed on paper for 136 years was gone in a week.

Beat 6 (the payoff): So what did the Velocity see? No one is sure. The best guess is pumice: floating rafts of volcanic rock that can drift for months, wide enough to look like land from a deck, across waters the scientists call a pumice superhighway. An honest mistake in 1876, copied faithfully for 136 years, until a ship went and looked. The lesson: every map is somebody's claim.

## The deep sea / profile: The Vampire Squid

Hook: Ravi, this animal lives on almost no oxygen and barely moves, and it still does fine; keep that in mind when your own breath gets short on the hill.

Beat 1 (the name): Its scientific name, Vampyroteuthis infernalis, means vampire squid from hell, and it is neither a vampire nor really a squid. It is about the size, shape and colour of a football, dark red, with enormous blue eyes. It grows to about thirty centimetres in total length, a foot, at most.

Beat 2 (the home): It lives in the oxygen minimum zone, a layer of deep ocean where there is almost no oxygen. Most animals cannot stay there, which is the point: few predators follow it in. It is thought to live at roughly six hundred to nine hundred metres, about two to three thousand feet, or deeper (depth is single source).

Beat 3 (the surprise): For years people assumed such a creature must hunt. In 2012 researchers Henk-Jan Hoving and Bruce Robison, working from Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute footage, showed it is a detritus eater. It puts out thread-like filaments, up to eight times its body length, to catch marine snow: the drifting mix of dead bodies, droppings and mucus sinking from above.

Beat 4 (the method): The filaments are sticky. The squid draws one through its arms, wraps the gathered particles in mucus, and eats the glob. The team had reviewed 170 video clips, over twenty-three hours, from twenty-five years of remotely operated vehicle dives to see it. It is a patient filter-feeder of a falling snowstorm, not a hunter.

Beat 5 (the defence): When threatened, it pulls its cloak-like web of skin up over itself and turns inside out, showing rows of soft spines, in a pose often called the pineapple. It was first described in 1903 by the German scientist Carl Chun from the Valdivia expedition, who at first took it for an octopus (single source). Today it is the only living member of its group.

## The history of maps / myth_or_fact: "Here Be Dragons"

Hook: Ravi, you have probably heard that old maps warned sailors "here be dragons" past the edge of the known world; do your legs believe it? Verdict: mostly myth.

Beat 1 (the claim): Picture an old map with a blank edge, a sea serpent, and the warning "here be dragons". It is one of the best-loved facts about maps. The verdict: mostly myth. Dragons and sea monsters really were drawn on old maps. The famous English phrase, as a warning to travellers, was not.

Beat 2 (the evidence): No English-language map carries the words. Only one map, or at most two, is known to carry the Latin version, "hic sunt dracones", here are dragons. The best-known is the Hunt-Lenox Globe, now in the New York Public Library: a copper globe about five inches across, dated anywhere from 1504 to about 1510, depending on the source.

Beat 3 (the second): Its dragons sit at the far edge of Asia, near the coast, as an eastern outpost of the unknown. A second globe, made from two ostrich eggs and shown at about 1504, is said to carry the same phrase and may be the prototype of the first (single source for the egg globe). Two small globes in the whole record: that is the entire evidence for the saying.

Beat 4 (the real phrase): What did mapmakers write over unknown land? "Hic sunt leones", here are lions, a phrase used from Roman times through the Middle Ages. Lions, not dragons. The popular dragon version seems to have come later, as a romantic memory of old maps, and it is hard to trace where it began.

Beat 5 (the real monsters): But monsters were everywhere. The Carta Marina of 1539, by the Swedish cartographer Olaus Magnus, took twelve years to make and is crowded with sea creatures. The Fra Mauro map of about 1450 marks an Island of Dragons in the Atlantic, and the thirteenth-century Ebstorf map has a dragon in south-east Africa. So: monsters yes, the famous warning no.
