## Space history / number: Two thousand and forty-eight words

Hook: Jo, you pack light for every trip, and the computer that flew Apollo to the Moon packed lighter still.

- The Apollo Guidance Computer had just 2,048 words of erasable memory, the space where it kept its live calculations. Its fixed memory, woven by hand as core rope, held 36,864 words of programs. Each word was sixteen bits: fifteen of data and one parity check. The whole machine weighed about 70 pounds.
- During Apollo 11's descent, it flashed a 1202 alarm, executive overflow: more jobs than it had room to track. A 1201 followed. The cause was the rendezvous radar, left on standby, whose stray signals added roughly 13 percent load to a processor already running near 85 percent.
- The software had been built to cope. Its priority scheduling dropped the lower-priority tasks, including a display, and kept the guidance jobs running. In Houston, guidance controller Steve Bales and his team, including Jack Garman, made the call: go. The landing went ahead.

## Space history / myth_or_fact: Did lightning end the Apollo 12 launch?

Hook: Jo, you know the Apollo 13 film; the crew before them nearly lost their mission thirty-six seconds into the climb.

- Myth: a lightning strike means the launch is over. Fact: Apollo 12 left Kennedy Space Center on November 14, 1969, in rain and overcast. At 36.5 seconds after liftoff, about 6,000 feet up, lightning hit. The surge knocked all three fuel cells offline, and the spacecraft fell back on batteries.
- Fifty-two seconds after liftoff, a second strike hit. It knocked out the attitude indicator, the "8-ball," and garbled the data streaming to Mission Control. The Saturn V's own guidance was untouched and the rocket kept flying. The crew saw a board of warning lights and could not tell what was wrong.
- In Houston, flight controller John Aaron recognized the garbled pattern from an earlier test, and called for the signal conditioning electronics to be switched to auxiliary. Commander Pete Conrad did not know that switch. Lunar module pilot Alan Bean did, and threw it. Data came back, Bean restored the fuel cells, and the mission flew on to the Moon.

## Jazz / explainer: Kind of Blue, sketches instead of scores

Hook: Jo, you loved Kind of Blue; here is how little was written down when the band played it.

- Kind of Blue was recorded at Columbia's 30th Street Studio in New York City, on three-track tape, in two sessions: March 2 and April 22, 1959. The first gave "So What," "Freddie Freeloader" and "Blue in Green." The second gave "All Blues" and "Flamenco Sketches."
- Miles Davis did not hand out finished scores. He brought sketches: scales and melody lines to improvise on, with brief instructions for each piece and almost no rehearsal. Pianist Bill Evans wrote in the liner notes that Davis conceived these settings only hours before the sessions.
- The band: Davis on trumpet, John Coltrane on tenor saxophone, Cannonball Adderley on alto, Bill Evans on piano, Paul Chambers on bass and Jimmy Cobb on drums. Wynton Kelly played piano on "Freddie Freeloader." The legend that it was all captured in one pass is untrue, but the sketched-out freshness is real.
