## Aviation history / explainer: How the X-1 got through the sound barrier

Hook: Frank, hold this steady leg work and picture a pilot holding a plane steady while the air itself changes the rules.

- On October 14, 1947, Captain Chuck Yeager flew the Bell X-1, which he had named Glamorous Glennis, over the Mojave Desert near Muroc Dry Lake in California. A B-29 carried it up and dropped it from its bomb bay. Then a rocket engine pushed the little bullet-shaped plane onward.
- The trouble was the air, not the engine. NASA's Langley engineers called compressibility the primary problem: as speed nears the speed of sound, shock waves blossom and multiply over the aircraft. Earlier attempts met severe buffeting near that speed, and the controls lost their bite.
- The fix was a horizontal stabilizer the pilot could adjust in flight. Engineers had upgraded it so Yeager could change its angle in small, instant steps. That smoothed the airflow near the speed of sound and kept the elevators effective, so he could still steer the nose.
- The striking part: on his ninth powered flight the Mach meter jumped from 0.965 to 1.06, about 700 miles an hour. The fuselage was shaped like a fifty-caliber bullet. The Smithsonian calls the sound barrier more myth than physical wall; it was a control problem, solved.

## Aviation history / number: 0.0033, the figure the Wright brothers fixed

Hook: Frank, a tiny number, held steady, just like your tempo on this set.

- From September to December 1901, Wilbur and Orville Wright ran wind tunnel tests on wing shapes. They were checking the published numbers that engineers trusted for lift, because their glider's performance would depend on those numbers being right.
- One number was the Smeaton coefficient. English engineer John Smeaton worked it out in the eighteenth century for designing windmills, and aviators had accepted it as true for almost one hundred fifty years.
- The Wrights found that a value of 0.0033 matched their data better. The value accepted today is 0.00326, so the brothers landed within a hair of it, after years of everyone trusting the old figure.
- By mid-December 1901 they concluded that the old Lilienthal lift tables were largely sound, and that the long-trusted coefficient was the culprit. They had corrected a number that had stood for generations.

## Crime novels / profile: Raymond Chandler, the writer who cannibalized himself

Hook: Frank, Chandler built his first novel the way you build a set: reuse what already works, one piece at a time.

- Raymond Chandler published his first novel, The Big Sleep, in 1939. It was not written from scratch. Its core came from two short stories he had already sold to the pulp magazine Black Mask: Killer in the Rain in 1935 and The Curtain in 1936.
- Chandler had a word for this: cannibalizing. He took chapters and scenes from his published stories and reworked them so they ran together as one novel, with his detective, Philip Marlowe, carrying the plot from page to page.
- He also borrowed small pieces from two other stories, Finger Man and Mandarin's Jade. He kept cannibalizing for later novels, too.
- A twist for the ear: he later asked that eight of the stories he had stripped for parts be left out of his collections. They only reached print together after his death, in a book called Killer in the Rain, in 1964.
