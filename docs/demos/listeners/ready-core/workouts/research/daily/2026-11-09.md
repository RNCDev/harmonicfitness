## How the body works / number: About twenty thousand breaths a day

Hook: the brace starts with the breath, and the muscle doing the breathing is the roof of your core.

- A healthy adult at rest breathes about twelve to twenty times a minute. Over a day that comes to something like twenty thousand breaths (the range runs from about seventeen thousand to well over twenty thousand, and more when you're active).
- The diaphragm, the dome-shaped muscle under the ribs, does most of the work of a quiet breath.
- Told as: "about twenty thousand a day", a round figure; never an exact count.

Source: https://wonderopolis.org/wonder/how-many-breaths-do-you-take-each-day
Source: https://redorbit.com/How-Many-Breaths-per-Minute-Is-Average-for-an-Adult (twelve to twenty a minute, American Thoracic Society and WHO figures)

## How the body works / myth_or_fact: We have five senses

Hook: the sense you lean on most in a bird dog isn't on the famous list.

- The claim: humans have five senses, sight, hearing, touch, taste and smell. The list goes back to Aristotle, around three hundred and fifty BC, and is still the everyday one.
- The answer: more myth than fact. The list leaves out balance (the vestibular system in the inner ear) and proprioception, the sense of where the parts of your body are and how they're moving. The word proprioception was coined by the physiologist Charles Sherrington in nineteen oh six.
- How many there are depends on how you count: some researchers count dozens. Told as: "at least seven", balance and the body's sense of its own position added to the five.

Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2024.1404720/full (Brandt, Dieterich and Huppert, "Human senses and sensors from Aristotle to the present", Frontiers in Neurology, 2024)
Source: https://www.vice.com/en/article/humans-dont-have-5-senses-they-have-as-many-as-33/

## Remarkable feats / explainer: Blondin, the rope over Niagara, and the pole

Hook: a tightrope walker's pole works the way a steady middle does: it buys time to correct a wobble.

- In eighteen fifty-nine Charles Blondin, a French acrobat, walked a rope strung across the Niagara Gorge below the falls: about eleven hundred feet long, about a hundred and sixty feet above the water. He crossed it many times after that, blindfolded, pushing a wheelbarrow, on stilts, and once he sat down halfway and cooked an omelette.
- That same summer he carried his manager, Harry Colcord, across on his back.
- Why the long pole helps: its weight sits far out from the body, which makes the walker much harder to tip. A wobble develops more slowly, so there's time to feel it and correct it.
- Left out: the date of the Colcord crossing (sources give the seventeenth and nineteenth of August), the rope's height on that day (one source says a hundred and ninety feet), and a quote often attributed to Blondin ("if I sway, sway with me"), which no reliable source confirms.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Blondin
Source: https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-tightrope-walk-over-niagara-falls (eleven hundred feet, a hundred and sixty feet up)
Source: https://blondinmemorialtrust.com/blondin-feats.html (carried Colcord in August eighteen fifty-nine)
Source: https://plus.maths.org/content/walking-tightrope (the pole raises the moment of inertia: wobbles develop slowly enough to respond)
Source: https://www.gresham.ac.uk/node/12039 (the pole is about inertia, not centre of gravity)

## Animals and nature / explainer: How a falling cat turns over

Hook: the cat's trick is the rotation work in today's punches: the top half and the bottom half turning separately.

- In eighteen ninety-four the French scientist Étienne-Jules Marey filmed a cat dropped upside down, with a camera taking rapid photographs in sequence, twelve a second. His pictures showed it began falling with no spin at all, so it wasn't pushing off the hand that dropped it, and still landed on its feet.
- How: the cat bends at the middle and turns its two halves separately. With the front legs tucked in, the front half turns first; then the front half becomes the anchor and the back half follows. Physicists later modelled the cat as two cylinders, front and back, turning against each other.
- Left out: the NASA and astronaut connection (one source only).

## Animals and nature / memory_game: Five animals that can outlive us

Hook: five things to hold through the workout; the link between them is the payoff at the end.

- The list: a clam, a shark, a whale, a tortoise and a sea urchin. Each, in its species, can live far beyond a human lifetime: the ocean quahog clam, the Greenland shark, the bowhead whale, the giant tortoise, the red sea urchin.
- The record: an ocean quahog clam dredged off the north coast of Iceland in two thousand and six was found, by counting the yearly growth bands in its shell, to be five hundred and seven years old. It was nicknamed Ming, after the Chinese dynasty it was born under.
- Told at the recall: the five, the link (all can outlive a person), and Ming's five hundred and seven years. No other figures.
