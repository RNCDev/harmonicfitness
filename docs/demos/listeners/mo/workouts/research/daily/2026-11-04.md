## Brain teasers / brain_teaser: Sixty-three matches

Hook: Mo, a sixty-four-team cup is a bracket you can run in your head while the bell swings, and the answer is a one-line trick.

Candidate A (the key one). Said aloud: "A cup has sixty-four teams. Lose once and you're out. Every round, the winners go through, until one team is left. How many matches are played in all? Most people start adding thirty-two, sixteen, eight. There's a faster way."

- Answer: sixty-three. Each match sends home exactly one team, and sixty-three teams must go home for one champion to remain. So count the losers, not the games.
- Reasoning a listener can follow: the long way works too, thirty-two plus sixteen plus eight plus four plus two plus one, six rounds, still sixty-three. The shortcut works for any number of teams: one fewer match than teams.
- Extension if time allows: with a play-in, the same rule holds. Sixty-eight teams in a bracket need sixty-seven matches, four of them play-in games.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-elimination_tournament
Source: none second opened; the answer is a counting proof (each match eliminates one team), the Wikipedia page confirms the loser is out at once and gives the six-round structure. Treat as self-proving.

Candidate B (chess, with a legend). Said aloud: "The legend says chess's inventor asked his king for a little wheat. One grain on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, doubling on every square. The king laughed at how small the request was. How many grains on the last square, the sixty-fourth?" Spoken answer: nine quintillion, two hundred twenty-three quadrillion, and change, more than the first sixty-three squares put together by one grain. Too many digits to say; better framed as "Is the last square's pile bigger than all the others added up?" Yes, by exactly one grain.

- The legend: the inventor, called Sissa in some tellings, asks the ruler of India for wheat that doubles on each square of the board. The treasurers find the total beyond the kingdom's means. Tellings differ on whether the inventor is rewarded or executed.
- The numbers: the full board holds two to the sixty-fourth, minus one, grains, 18,446,744,073,709,551,615. The first half of the board holds 4,294,967,295 grains, about 279 tonnes at 65 milligrams a grain. That is more grains than existed, and by Wikipedia's estimate over 1,400 times recent yearly world wheat production.
- Why it clicks: each square's pile is one more than everything before it, so the last pile alone beats all the others combined, by one grain. Doubling feels slow and then is not.
- Caution for the writer: the earliest source is disputed (Wikipedia gives 1256 by Ibn Khallikan; ProofWiki's note names no source for the legend and cites an 1881 Victorian book for a modernised pin version). Say "an old legend", give no date. ProofWiki names the king as Shirham and the inventor Sissa ben Dahir; Wikipedia says only "a ruler of India". Say "the king".

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat_and_chessboard_problem
Source: https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Wheat_and_Chessboard_Problem/Historical_Note

Candidate C (chess, by colour). Said aloud: "Take a chessboard and snip off two opposite corners. Sixty-two squares are left. You have thirty-one dominoes, each covering two touching squares. Can you cover the whole board exactly?" Answer: no. Opposite corners are the same colour, so you removed two of one colour. That leaves thirty-two squares of one colour and thirty of the other, and every domino covers one of each.

- Origin: the philosopher Max Black posed it in his 1946 book Critical Thinking, with a nudge toward the colouring answer. It was popularised in the 1950s by Martin Gardner in Scientific American (1957) and others.
- Why it clicks: no diagram needed; say "dark and light" and the listener sees the two corners on one diagonal. Many people try placing dominoes for a full minute before the colour idea lands, which suits a one-minute set.
- Only one source opened for this candidate (budget); a second would be needed before it is used as the key item.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutilated_chessboard_problem
Source: none second opened (not confirmed twice)

Recommendation: use A as the spoken puzzle (cleanest, two or three sentences, answer in one line), keep B as the follow-up in the rest, hold C in reserve.

## Chess history / profile: Kasparov and the machine, 1996 and 1997

Hook: Mo, if the Queen's Gambit was one brilliant player against the world's best, this is the real version: the world champion against a computer built to beat him.

- In February 1996, in Philadelphia, world champion Garry Kasparov played IBM's Deep Blue over six games, from the tenth to the seventeenth. Deep Blue won the first game. Kasparov then won the second, drew the third and fourth, won the fifth and sixth, and took the match four points to two.
- A year later, in May 1997 in New York, they met again with an upgraded machine. Kasparov won game one. Deep Blue won game two, and Kasparov resigned a position that later analysis suggested he might have held. Games three, four and five were draws, so the match stood level going into the last game.
- Game six, on the eleventh of May, was over quickly. Kasparov, as Black, chose the Caro-Kann Defence, a solid opening. Deep Blue sacrificed a knight that wrecked his defence, and he resigned after only nineteen moves. Deep Blue took the match three and a half to two and a half.
- The machine searched on a scale no person can: the 1996 version could look at about a hundred million positions a second, and the 1997 version about two hundred million.- Kasparov has said since that after researching the matches, he respected the Deep Blue team more, and thought both his play and the machine's were weaker than the legend suggests.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Blue_versus_Garry_Kasparov
Source: https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/kasparov-defeats-chess-playing-computer

Notes for the writer: both pages confirm the 1996 match (Kasparov 4 to 2, won game 1 for Deep Blue), the 1997 score (3.5 to 2.5), game one Kasparov, game two Deep Blue, games three to five drawn, and game six Deep Blue on 11 May. Only Wikipedia gives the Caro-Kann, the nineteen moves, the knight sacrifice, the Philadelphia and New York locations, the "could have held" and the Kasparov hindsight remark; only History.com gives the positions-per-second figures. Use those lightly, or say "about nineteen moves". Avoid saying "first computer to beat a world champion in a match": that is only true of the 1997 match; the 1996 win was one game. Avoid prize money.

## Football tactics / explainer: Where total football came from

Hook: Mo, Inverting the Pyramid is about ideas passed hand to hand over generations; this is one chain of it, from an English coach in Amsterdam to the Dutch side of the seventies.

- The idea: in total football, any outfield player can take over another's job, and when one moves out of position a teammate fills the gap, so the team's shape holds. A full-back overlaps, a striker drops deep, and the whole team moves as one unit.
- It did not arrive in one piece. Jack Reynolds, an English manager at Ajax, used a similar style in the 1940s that helped the club rise and win its first trophies. Vic Buckingham, another English coach, managed West Brom, Ajax and Barcelona in the 1950s and 1960s, and his teaching shaped the same school.
- Rinus Michels took over Ajax in January 1965. As a player he had learned under Reynolds and Buckingham, and he turned those habits into a system, built around a roaming centre forward, Johan Cruyff, who drifted to wherever the space was.
- The pieces that made it work: stretch the pitch when you have the ball, squeeze the space when you lose it, and press the ball carrier at once while marking his nearest passing options. Michels's team made the field big for itself and small for the opposition.
- The idea reached its biggest stage with the Netherlands at the 1974 World Cup, where they beat Argentina and Brazil along the way, and lost the final two to one to West Germany. They lost, yet the style was the one people remembered, and it later shaped Barcelona's possession game under Pep Guardiola.
