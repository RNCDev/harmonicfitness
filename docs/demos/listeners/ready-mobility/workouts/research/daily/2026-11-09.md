## How the body works: the surprising science of muscles, breath, balance and sleep / profile: The Doctor Who Watched the Bus Stairs

Hook: for anyone who sits all day, the man who first showed that a job on your feet is kinder to the heart than a job in a seat.

- Jerry Morris (Jeremy Morris) was born in Liverpool in 1910 and grew up poor in Glasgow. He became a doctor and an epidemiologist, and in 1949 he looked at heart disease among London's busmen, drivers and conductors of the same social class,.
- The drivers sat for almost the whole shift. The conductors climbed up and down the stairs collecting fares, about five hundred to seven hundred and fifty steps a working day. The conductors were about half as likely as the drivers to have a sudden heart attack. The paper came out in The Lancet in 1953, and it is described as a turning point in linking physical activity and health.
- He lived it. He jogged on Hampstead Heath when that was thought peculiar, took the stairs to his office at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine into his eighties, and was still exercising in his hundredth year. He died aged ninety-nine.

## Animals and nature: the strange and wonderful things they do / explainer: How an Owl Turns Its Head Without Hurting Itself

Hook: while you turn gently through your own spine, the bird that turns three-quarters of the way round.

- Many owls can turn their heads about two hundred and seventy degrees each way. They have to: their eyes are long and tube-shaped, built for seeing in the dark, and they can't roll them the way we roll ours. An owl's neck has fourteen vertebrae, twice our seven.
- The puzzle was the blood. Twisting a neck that far should pinch the arteries to the brain. In 2013 a team at Johns Hopkins, the medical illustrator Fabian de Kok-Mercado and the neuroradiologist Philippe Gailloud, studied a dozen owls that had died naturally, with dye and scans, and published in Science.
- What they found: the holes in the neck bones that the arteries run through are about ten times wider than the artery itself, leaving room to move. The vessels under the jaw widen into little reservoirs that pool blood, and small cross-links let blood take another route if one is pinched. Gailloud said humans lack many of these protections.

## Everyday science: why ordinary things work the way they do / explainer: Why You're Taller in the Morning

Hook: for a stiff back after a day in a chair, why the spine is a little shorter at night and a little longer in the morning.

- Between the bones of the spine are discs, shock absorbers with a tough outer ring and a soft, water-rich middle. Standing, walking and sitting load the spine and squeeze some fluid out of them. Lying down takes the load off, and the fluid seeps back in.
- So most people are measurably taller when they wake than at bedtime, often around a centimetre, sometimes more; one source says up to an inch. Most of the loss happens in the first few hours after getting up.
- Take gravity away and it goes further. NASA scientists say astronauts can grow up to about three percent taller in orbit, around two inches for a six-foot person, because the spine is no longer pressed down. It's temporary: back on Earth, they return to their usual height.

## Remarkable feats: explorers, record-setters and the stories behind them / profile: Nellie Bly and the One Small Bag

Hook: a whole-world trip packed into one small bag, for a session that needs only a chair and a towel.

- Nellie Bly, born Elizabeth Cochran, was a young reporter at the New York World. She pitched beating the eighty days of Phileas Fogg, Jules Verne's fictional traveller. The business manager wanted to send a man, who would need no chaperone and none of the dozen trunks a woman would bring. Her answer: start the man, and she'd start the same day for another newspaper and beat him.
- She sailed from Hoboken in eighteen eighty-nine wearing her only dress, with one bag, sixteen inches wide and seven high, holding two caps, three veils and a jar of cold cream, among a few other small things. In France she travelled day and night to meet Verne at his home in Amiens.
- She did it in seventy-two days, six hours, eleven minutes and fourteen seconds. A rival magazine, Cosmopolitan, had sent Elizabeth Bisland round the other way the same day; Bly only learned of the race in Hong Kong, and Bisland came in about four and a half days after her.
