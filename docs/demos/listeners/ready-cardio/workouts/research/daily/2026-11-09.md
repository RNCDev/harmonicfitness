## Remarkable feats, Animals and nature / serial: The serum run to Nome, and the dog who ran the hardest miles

Hook: the most famous dog in the story isn't the one who ran the hardest miles.

- The town. In 1925 Nome, Alaska, was cut off: the port frozen, no road in. Its only doctor, Curtis Welch, found diphtheria, and the hospital's antitoxin had expired. The nearest serum, 300,000 units, was found in an Anchorage hospital, a package of a little over twenty pounds, sent by train to Nenana.
- The relay. From Nenana to Nome, 674 miles, by 20 mushers and about 150 dogs, in five and a half days (127 and a half hours). The first musher left at night at about fifty below, and it fell to about sixty-two below. The earlier record for the trail was nine days (Mental Floss).
- Togo. Leonhard Seppala's lead dog, twelve years old, about forty-eight pounds as an adult. As a puppy he was sickly and mischievous; Seppala gave him away as a pet at about six months, and after a few weeks he leapt through a closed window and ran miles back to the kennel. Seppala kept him.
- Norton Sound. Seppala and Togo ran about 170 miles out from Nome to meet the serum, then turned back and, to save time, crossed about twenty miles of open sea ice on Norton Sound in a gale, wind chill about eighty-five below. While they slept that night, the ice they had just crossed blew out to sea (Wikipedia: "it had blown out to sea all the ice Seppala had just crossed"; Mental Floss: the ice broke up completely hours after they crossed).
- The distance. Seppala and Togo covered about 261 miles in all (Wikipedia, Mental Floss; AKC says 264), 91 of them carrying the serum. Other teams averaged about 31 miles (AKC); no other team ran more than 53 (Seppala site). Handoff at Golovin, 78 miles from Nome.
- The last leg. Gunnar Kaasen, lead dog Balto, about 53 miles (some say 55), through a blizzard so thick Kaasen couldn't always see the dogs nearest the sled; Balto kept to the trail. The sled flipped and the serum was thrown into the snow; Kaasen searched on his hands and knees, bare-handed, and got frostbite, and found it. They reached Front Street in Nome at five thirty in the morning. Not one vial was broken.
- After. A statue of Balto was unveiled in Central Park the same year (December 1925). Seppala was bitter that Balto got the credit. Togo got his own statue in New York in 2001 (now in Seward Park), and his mounted body is in the Iditarod headquarters museum in Wasilla, Alaska.
- Togo's last years: in Maine, fathering puppies; died at sixteen in 1929 (Mental Floss; Wikipedia, Togo; Maine Memory Network).
- Left out on purpose: the earlier record for the trail (only Mental Floss gives nine days), the exact dates (sources differ on February 2 or 3), the ice-floe swim (Wikipedia places it on an earlier trip, AKC in the serum run), Norton Sound's width (20 or 42 miles), the death toll.

## Animals and nature / this_or_that: The cheetah or the sled dog

Hook: if you could borrow one animal's engine for this workout, which one?

- The cheetah. The fastest land animal: about seventy miles an hour (Columbus Zoo: 70; Smithsonian National Zoo's fact sheet: up to 64, only briefly sustained). It keeps that speed only for a short distance, about 400 to 600 yards (Columbus Zoo), and a chase is usually over within a minute (Columbus Zoo; National Geographic: an average of 38 seconds). Afterwards it needs a long rest before it can chase again (Columbus Zoo: up to twenty minutes; Kruger Park: about half an hour).
- The sled dog. Nowhere near as fast, but it can keep going for days in the cold: in the serum run, Togo ran about 261 miles in a few days at twelve years old (see the serial).
- The coach's pick: the sled dog. A cheetah's sprint is about as long as today's hard intervals; today trains a little of both.

## How the body works / quiz: How many times does your heart beat in a day?

Hook: a question you can work out in your head while you march.

- The question: roughly how many times does an adult heart beat in a day: ten thousand, a hundred thousand, or a million?
- The answer: about a hundred thousand (PeaceHealth: "Each day, the average heart beats 100,000 times"; Willamette Valley Medical Center, citing the American Heart Association: "Your adult heart beats about 100,000 times a day").
- The working: a resting heart beats between sixty and a hundred times a minute (PeaceHealth; the American Heart Association's range). About seventy a minute, times the 1,440 minutes in a day, is about a hundred thousand.
