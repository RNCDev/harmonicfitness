## Remarkable feats / how_they_trained: How astronauts squat with no gravity

Hook: everyone listening has a free training partner astronauts have to build: gravity.

- Without Earth's gravity, both bone and muscle shrink and weaken. To hold on to them, crews on the International Space Station now average about two hours of exercise a day (NASA). The Canadian Space Agency gives the same two-hour routine, and says some astronauts have come home with more muscle than they left with, though results vary.
- A squat with no weight to lift does nothing, so NASA built a machine to make one. The Advanced Resistive Exercise Device, installed on the station in the late two thousands, mimics free weights with vacuum cylinders, with pistons inside them, and a flywheel that recreates the feel of a weight's inertia. Crews do squats, deadlifts and calf raises on it.
- The bar loads up to about six hundred pounds of force, twice its predecessor. Link to today: astronauts need a machine to squat; we have gravity and a chair.

## Everyday science / explainer: Why small things are strong for their size

Hook: why an ant is a better lifter, for its size, than any of us, and who worked it out.

- The question: why do small creatures seem so strong for their size? Galileo answered it in his last book, Two New Sciences, in sixteen thirty-eight. A muscle's or a beam's strength depends on its cross-section, its area, while its weight depends on its volume. Grow anything bigger and the volume outruns the area.
- Double every measurement of a shape and its area goes up four times, but its volume eight times (Wikipedia's cube: six square metres to twenty-four, one cubic metre to eight). Scale an animal up and muscle cross-section grows with the square, mass with the cube, so it gets relatively weaker. Galileo's line: the smaller the body, the greater its relative strength. A giant with ordinary proportions would be crushed by its own weight.
- An ant shows it. Engineers at Ohio State found the neck joint of the Allegheny mound ant held between about three thousand four hundred and five thousand times the ant's body weight before it failed (Journal of Biomechanics, twenty fourteen). In one sentence: strength grows with the square, weight with the cube.

Source: https://scientificamerican.com/article/388-years-ago-galileo-worked-out-why-human-giants-cant-exist-and-explained-a-law-of-nature (Two New Sciences, 1638; strength by cross-section, weight by volume; "the smaller the body the greater its relative strength"; giants crushed)
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square%E2%80%93cube_law (first in Two New Sciences, 1638; doubling a cube's side: area 6 to 24, volume 1 to 8; muscle cross-section with the square, mass with the cube, so smaller animals relatively stronger)

## How the body works / explainer: Why the first weeks of strength come from the nervous system

Hook: why the second time through a new movement already feels steadier.

- In the first weeks of strength training, much of the new strength comes before the muscles grow. The nervous system gets better at using the muscle already there: more of it switched on, firing harder and more together. A review in twenty twenty-one puts the window at roughly the first two to four weeks; muscle growth takes over later.
- The classic study: Moritani and de Vries, nineteen seventy-nine, eight weeks of training in young men and women; neural factors accounted for the larger share of the early strength gain, and muscle growth became the bigger part later. Wikipedia: the first big improvements in new lifters come from increased neural drive, separate from muscle growth. (Reviews note the exact mechanism is still debated: tell it as "much of", never "all".)
- A side note from the same review: training one limb raises the strength of the same muscle in the other limb, cross-education, one of the clues that the nervous system is learning. In one sentence: early on, your body is learning the movement as much as building muscle for it.

Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7892509 (initial, under two to four weeks, force gains primarily neural; cross-education)
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strength_training ("initial significant improvements in strength amongst new lifters are a result of increased neural drive", separate from hypertrophy)
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/453338/ (Moritani and de Vries, 1979, eight weeks; neural factors the larger share early, hypertrophy later; from the abstract in search results)

## - / brain_teaser: A brick and a half

Hook: a lifting puzzle that needs no pencil.

- The teaser, said slowly, twice: a brick weighs one kilo plus half a brick. How much does the whole brick weigh?
- Answer: two kilos. How to get there: the brick is one kilo plus half a brick, so the other half of the brick must be that one kilo. Half a brick is one kilo, so the whole brick is two. The trap is answering one and a half.

## Animals and nature / brain_teaser: The lily pads

Hook: a pond puzzle where the first answer is the wrong one.

- The teaser, said slowly, twice: a patch of lily pads doubles in size every day. After forty-eight days it covers the whole lake. How many days did it take to cover half the lake?
- Answer: forty-seven. The patch doubles every day, so the day before it covered the whole lake it covered exactly half. The answer that jumps out first is twenty-four. It is one of the three questions in Shane Frederick's Cognitive Reflection Test (Journal of Economic Perspectives, two thousand five).

## Remarkable feats / how_they_trained: Milo and the calf

Hook: the oldest story in strength training, and the whole idea of getting stronger in one picture.

- Milo of Croton was a wrestler in the Greek world of the sixth century BC. Wikipedia counts six Olympic wins: the boys' wrestling in 540 BC, then the men's five times, at the 62nd to 66th Olympics. History Skills lists the same six dates (540, then 536 to 520 BCE). History.com gives ten titles, likely counting other festivals, so the script says "again and again", not a count.
- The legend: he carried a newborn calf on his shoulders every day, and as the calf grew, so did his strength. Four years on it was a full-grown animal (Wikipedia: a four-year-old cow; History Skills: a full-grown bull) and he could still lift it. A version has him carrying it the length of the stadium at Olympia, then eating it (History.com: a four-year-old bull around the stadium, eaten in one day).
- All three sources present it as legend: "according to legend", "as one story goes", "it's impossible to know which feats were real". Link to today: a little more than last time, every time.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milo_of_Croton (six wins with dates; newborn calf carried daily until the Olympics, by then a four-year-old cow; carried the length of the stadium)
Source: https://historyskills.com/classroom/year-7/milo-of-croton (as one story goes, the calf carried each day grew into a full-grown bull after four years; titles around 540 and 536 to 520 BCE)
Source: https://www.history.com/articles/worlds-strongest-men-ancient-greece-rome (four-year-old bull carried around the stadium at Olympia; sixth century BC; feats impossible to verify)
