## Mountaineering / how_they_trained: How Alex Honnold trained for the Freerider solo

Hook: Lena grinds through weighted work, and the man in her favourite film built the hardest climb of his life on bodyweight hangs, a van doorframe and a year of rehearsal.

- Alex Honnold thought about soloing Freerider on El Capitan for nearly a decade, then spent more than a year and a half planning and training. Freerider is about thirty pitches, roughly three thousand feet of granite. He climbed it on ropes for hundreds of hours across about a year, memorising every hand and foot sequence.
- His finger training was plain. A wooden Beastmaker 2000 hangboard sat in the doorway of his van. Each grip position: seven hangs of seven seconds, three seconds of rest between them. Six or seven grip positions per workout, repeated. No added weight, because he said the holds were already way too hard for him. He trained every other day.
- He followed the Beastmaker app's benchmark workouts for the last two months. Two months out, he could not finish the easiest one, the 6c workout. By the climb he was working through the harder 7b workout, which adds a seventh grip. A bad ankle sprain about two months before his first attempt cost him time, and the work carried on.
- He backed off in November 2016, around four fifty in the morning, at the Freeblast section. On June 3, 2017, he went again and finished in under four hours, with no rope. Detail for a lifter: the headline was hundreds of rehearsal hours and boring, repeated, small-dose hangs, not one heroic session.

## Formula One engineering / explainer: Brabham's fan car, the loophole that won once

Hook: Lena knows what an unfair advantage feels like on the bar; this car used one sentence in the rulebook and won its only race by more than thirty seconds.

- In 1978 the rules banned movable aerodynamic devices, but allowed cooling. Brabham's designer Gordon Murray added a big fan at the back of the BT46B and called it a radiator fan. Driven from the engine and gearbox, it also pulled air from under the car, and flexible skirts sealed the sides to the road.
- That meant extra downforce, the car pressed onto the track by suction, rather than by wings alone. The faster the engine turned, the stronger the pull. Brabham's argument was that the fan's main job was cooling, which was legal. Rivals Lotus, McLaren, Tyrrell, Surtees and Williams argued it was clearly aerodynamic.
- Five protests were lodged before the Swedish Grand Prix at Anderstorp on June 17, 1978, and the organisers rejected them all. Niki Lauda, second on the grid, won by more than half a minute, ahead of Riccardo Patrese. His teammate John Watson spun off. The race win stood, since the car was judged legal as raced.
- Brabham then withdrew it, even though the governing body had ruled it could be used for the rest of the season. Murray said team boss Bernie Ecclestone feared the row would split the teams' association. The fan car raced exactly once, and the loophole was closed before a planned twin-fan design could be built.

## Mountaineering / number: One third

Hook: Lena trains on a rep scheme where air is free; on Everest's summit every breath carries about a third of the oxygen it does at sea level, and in 1978 two men went up without a bottle.

- The number is about one third. At the summit of Everest, the oxygen pressure is roughly a third of what it is at sea level, which means each breath carries less than a third of the oxygen. The summit stands at about eight thousand eight hundred and forty-eight metres.
- In 1978, Peter Habeler and Reinhold Messner climbed Everest without any supplemental oxygen, breathing only that thin air all the way to the top. A reading taken on the summit by climber and filmmaker David Breashears recorded air pressure of about two hundred sixty millimetres of mercury, a figure NOVA published while noting it still awaited calibration.
- The detail for a lifter: every effort up there costs far more than the same effort at sea level, because each unit of work comes on about a third of the fuel in the air. Think of the hardest set you have done, then do it with a third of the oxygen.
