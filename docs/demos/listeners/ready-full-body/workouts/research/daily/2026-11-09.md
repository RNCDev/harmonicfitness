## How the body works / inside_the_move: Why you breathe harder when you move

Hook: the breath picks up in the warm-up, and it isn't mainly because oxygen is running low.

- Working muscles make carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide dissolved in the blood makes it slightly more acidic, and sensors on the surface of the brainstem (the medulla) pick up that change and turn the breathing up.
- Under ordinary conditions the main chemical signal that sets how hard you breathe is carbon dioxide, not oxygen. Oxygen has to fall a long way before the oxygen sensors in the neck (the carotid bodies) and by the heart (the aortic bodies) drive breathing much.
- So the puff in the warm-up is the body clearing what the muscles make. The talk test still applies: if you can't talk in sentences in the warm-up, slow down.

## How the body works / myth_or_fact: Cracking your knuckles does not cause arthritis

Hook: the warning everyone heard as a child, and the doctor who spent about fifty years testing it on himself.

- The claim: cracking your knuckles will give you arthritis in your fingers. No good evidence for it.
- Doctor Donald Unger cracked the knuckles of his left hand at least twice a day for about fifty years and left his right hand alone as the control. By his own count, the left hand was cracked at least thirty-six thousand five hundred times. He reported in a letter to the journal Arthritis and Rheumatism in 1998 that neither hand had arthritis and there was no apparent difference between them. It won him the Ig Nobel Prize in Medicine in 2009.
- One man is not a study, and a statistician pointed out it was not blinded. Larger studies agree, though: a 1990 study of three hundred people and a 2011 study of 215 people aged 50 to 89 found no link between knuckle cracking and arthritis in the hand.

## Animals and nature / number: Eleven days in the air

Hook: one young bird, a satellite tag, and a flight that never touched down.

- In October 2022 a juvenile bar-tailed godwit, about five months old, tagged 234684, left southwest Alaska and flew without stopping to Ansons Bay in northeastern Tasmania: at least 13,560 kilometres (8,435 miles) in 11 days and 1 hour. Guinness lists it as the longest non-stop migration by a bird (records can change, so the script doesn't call it a record).
- The Earth is about 40,075 kilometres (24,901 miles) around the equator (WGS 84; Wikipedia, Universe Today), so 8,435 miles is about a third of the way around the planet. The script says one distance only, in miles: more than eight thousand miles, about a third of the way around the world.
- Godwits fuel these flights with huge fat loads and shrink their digestive organs before leaving; the crossing is the longest journey without pausing to feed of any animal known on that route (Wikipedia). The researchers didn't know whether it flew alone or with a flock.

## How the body works / inside_the_move: The biggest muscle, and when it wakes up

Hook: the bridge works the gluteus maximus, the single largest muscle in the body.

- The gluteus maximus, the big muscle of each buttock, is the largest single muscle in the human body (Wikipedia; Cleveland Clinic: the biggest and strongest).
- Its size is one of the most characteristic features of human muscle; other primates have much flatter hips and can't sustain standing upright (Wikipedia), and its enlargement was likely important in the evolution of running (Lieberman 2006 abstract). The script says only: it sets us apart, other apes have much flatter hips, ours are built to stand tall and to run.
- A study by Daniel Lieberman and colleagues (2006, Journal of Experimental Biology) found it mostly quiet during ordinary level walking, and much more active in running. Cleveland Clinic: it is used when you need force to move forward, to stand up, run or climb.

## Animals and nature / number: A heart that beats twice a minute

Hook: for the slow breathing at the end, the slowest heartbeat ever measured in a blue whale.

- In 2019 Stanford's Jeremy Goldbogen and colleagues (PNAS, "Extreme bradycardia and tachycardia in the world's largest animal") attached a sensor tag, held on by suction cups with electrodes built in, to a wild blue whale in Monterey Bay (ScienceDaily). On dives its heart beat typically four to eight times a minute, and as low as two.
- Back at the surface, it rose to twenty-five to thirty-seven beats a minute.
- No human comparison figure was verified: the script compares only in words (much slower than yours, sitting still).

## Everyday science / myth_or_fact: Lightning is hotter than the surface of the sun (fact)

Hook: a question whose answer is "fact", so the session's two myth-or-facts don't both come out "myth".

- The claim: a bolt of lightning is hotter than the surface of the sun. True.
- The US National Weather Service: lightning can heat the air it passes through to 50,000 degrees Fahrenheit, five times hotter than the surface of the sun.
- National Geographic: lightning can heat the air around it to 50,000 degrees Fahrenheit, five times hotter than the sun's surface.
- The script says: up to about fifty thousand degrees Fahrenheit, around five times hotter than the surface of the sun. (The heat lasts only a flash; the script doesn't time it, since neither page says how long.)

## Remarkable feats / number: Seventy, and under three hours

Hook: the age at which a man who'd come back to running in his forties ran a marathon in under three hours.

- Ed Whitlock, of Milton, Ontario, became the first person over seventy to run a marathon in under three hours: 2:59:10 at age 72 at the 2003 Toronto Waterfront Marathon, then about 2:54 at 73 (Global News; Wikipedia; Scotiabank's tribute says he did it three times past seventy).
- He came back to running in his forties (Global News: rediscovered the sport in his 40s; Wikipedia: started running again at 41).
- He trained by running laps of the Evergreen Cemetery near his home in Milton, for hours (Global News; Athletics Illustrated / the search summary: he lived a hundred yards from it).
- The script says only: first person over seventy to run a marathon in under three hours; came back to running in his forties; trained on laps of the cemetery near his home, for hours at a time.
