# Session structure research

Scope: gaps not covered by knowledge/programming.md. Every cited source was fetched in this session; web-search summaries that I did not open are not cited. Where a fetch returned only an abstract/record, the source entry says so. Paraphrase is mine; quotes are short.

## 1. Block order and warm-up/cool-down scaling

- [2] HPRC (Uniformed Services/DoD performance site) reports that the NSCA "recommends using the following order of exercise categories in resistance-training workouts": power exercises, then other core or multi-joint exercises, then assistance or single-joint exercises. Within each category it suggests alternating upper- and lower-body lifts, or push and pull.
- [1] ACE (IFT model, phases 3-4 article): "Plyometric drills should be performed at the beginning of a training session after the completion of a dynamic warm-up." Plyometrics need a readiness gate (squat 1.5x bodyweight, or five squat reps at 60 percent of bodyweight in five seconds); start low intensity, "increase the volume ... before increasing intensity". Gate is for loaded plyometrics in the ACE model, not necessarily bodyweight hops.
- [1] ACE: sequencing methods include "grouping exercises based on body part". Rest rises with load: "The greater the load, the longer the rest period".
- [6] McGowan et al. 2015 review (abstract only): passive warm-up raises temperature without depleting energy stores; active warm-up produces greater metabolic changes; gives sport-specific recommendations. The abstract states no duration or intensity and no cool-down.
- Nothing found, from a source I opened, on how warm-up and cool-down minutes should scale with session length. See Not confirmed.

## 2. Method selection (straight sets, supersets, circuits, HIIT, EMOM, mobility)

- [5] Buchheit and Laursen 2013, Part I (abstract only): HIT is one of the most effective ways to improve cardiorespiratory and metabolic function; prescription involves "up to nine variables" (work and relief interval intensity and duration, modality, reps, series, between-series recovery); interest in protocols keeping athletes above 90 percent of VO2max. The abstract gives no work:rest figures.
- [9] Ramos-Campo et al. 2022 (abstract only; meta-analysis of 16 studies, middle-aged and older women): circuit resistance training reduced weight, BMI and fat mass, increased muscle mass, improved VO2max and strength, several gains larger than controls. It does not compare against traditional sets or report timing.
- [3] NASM blog: OPT phase 2 "pairs stable strength work with stabilization movements" (a superset-style method); phase 3 "drives muscle growth with higher volume"; phase 4 "develops peak force with heavier loads"; phase 5 "trains rate of force through explosive and plyometric work". The page gives reps only for phase 1 (about 12 to 20, slow controlled tempo) and no sets or rest.
- [1] ACE: "strive to complete two resistance-training sessions per week"; in phase 4 "the number of repetitions is reduced and the rest intervals are increased".
- No opened source covered EMOM, Tabata, or a mobility-session method. Straight-set/superset rest numbers are in programming.md (Iversen, ACSM).

## 3. Movement-pattern taxonomy and balance

- [1] ACE (phases 3-4 article): does not list the patterns; refers to "the basic patterns of exercise" and gives "push, pull or rotation" as an example grouping. (A search summary named bend-and-lift, single-leg, push, pull, rotation for ACE IFT phase 2, but I could not confirm it on a page I opened.)
- [7] Brown et al. 2024 (abstract only, Frontiers in Clinical Diabetes and Healthcare): proposes movement categories for resistance-training prescription: "hip, knee, ankle, vertebral column, vertical push, horizontal push, vertical pull, and horizontal pull." Does not use squat/hinge/carry/rotation labels.
- [8] Cook et al. 2014 (FMS Part 1, PMC): the FMS "is comprised of seven fundamental movement patterns"; Part 1 covers deep squat, hurdle step, in-line lunge only.
- [2] HPRC: "Resistance-training workouts should be balanced to include both push and pull exercises." "In a workout where you do 4-6 exercises, half of them should be pushes and the other half pulls." Upper/lower split is an accepted option; whole-body workouts are recommended for Service Members "since they are more functional" (as paraphrased from the page). It cites Ratamess (2017) and Sheppard and Triplett (2016).

## 4. Joint-load rating and regression/progression ladders

- [4] Escamilla 2001 review (Europe PMC record; I read a summary, not verbatim abstract): knee forces are lowest between roughly 0 and 50 degrees of knee flexion; muscle activity generally rises with flexion; squat is effective in cruciate and patellofemoral rehabilitation; healthy athletes should favor the parallel squat over the deep squat. Supports "shallower range = lower knee load" for squats only.
- [8] FMS: 0-3 scale (0 pain, 1 cannot complete, 2 completes with compensation, 3 completes correctly); "The lower score of the two sides is recorded"; scores "tell the professional when a person needs more investigation", corrective exercise follows, but the article gives no score-to-exercise mapping. A pain-gated screen, not a joint-load rating.
- [1] ACE: Phase 2 (basic patterns with control of centre of gravity over base of support) precedes load and power; plyometric progression is volume before intensity. No explicit regression ladder in the text (tables were images).
- [3] NASM: progression "is driven by the client's goals, readiness, and re-assessment, not a fixed calendar"; "Full range of motion and technique as the non-negotiable standard before load increases." Phases cycle rather than run one way.
- No published exercise-by-joint-load rating scheme for a general catalogue was confirmed.

## 5. Exercise count and time budgeting

- [2] HPRC: a workout of 4-6 exercises splits evenly between pushes and pulls. This is the only exercise-count figure I confirmed, and it applies to resistance work.
- Per-rep and per-set time estimates: nothing new confirmed. programming.md's "about two minutes per set with rest" is house arithmetic.

## Not confirmed

- Whether ACE's five patterns are bend-and-lift, single-leg, push, pull, rotation (search summary only). Likewise NASM's balance/core/resistance block order and the NSCA primary text; HPRC is a secondary summary.
- Squat/hinge/carry/locomotion/anti-rotation as a professional-body taxonomy: not found; Brown 2024 uses a different list.
- Work:rest shapes for EMOM, Tabata, circuits, and the Buchheit short vs long interval guidance (only the abstract record was readable). Tabata 1996 not retrieved.

## Synthesis (proposal, not findings)

Session table a tool could encode (blocks in order; times are house defaults, to be checked against programming.md):

| Method | Blocks | Goal fit | Time fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight/paired sets | warm-up, [power if gated], multi-joint, single-joint, breathing | strength, general | 25-60 |
| Superset/circuit | warm-up, 1-2 circuits of 4-6 moves, breathing | general fitness, conditioning | 15-45 |
| Interval/HIIT | easy lead-in, hard/easy rounds, easy down | conditioning | 10-45 |
| Mobility/breathing | easy warm-up, holds and range work, breathing | mobility | 15-45 |

Rules supported by sources: order power, then multi-joint, then single-joint [2]; plyometrics right after warm-up and gated by readiness [1]; roughly equal push and pull [2]; progress by readiness not calendar [3]; shallower squat range lowers knee load [4].

Pattern taxonomy to encode (mine; the source basis is partial): squat/knee-dominant, hinge/hip-dominant, single-leg, vertical push, horizontal push, vertical pull, horizontal pull, rotation/anti-rotation, carry, locomotion/conditioning. Splits: whole-body takes one from each lower pattern plus push and pull; upper/lower or push/pull targeted sessions take 4-6 moves balanced as in [2]. Tag each movement with a regression rung (range, support, one-sided, load), with pain-gated advancement as in [8].
