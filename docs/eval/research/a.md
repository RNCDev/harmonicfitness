The patch is about the size of Maplefield, roughly 9 kilometers across, near the moon's south pole.

Warm ice usually means something underneath is moving: heat leaking up through cracks from a liquid layer below. The mission team says it is the strongest hint yet that this moon holds a hidden ocean, though they are careful to say hint. Nothing has been seen spraying out, the way it does on Enceladus.

## Birdwatching / explainer: Telling sparrows apart by song this week

Late October is sparrow season in Maplefield's hedges, and Maya wants to learn birds by ear.

Two sparrows are passing through right now, and they look alike: small, brown, streaky. Their songs are nothing alike. The white-throated sparrow whistles a slow, clear tune birders spell out as "Old Sam Peabody, Peabody, Peabody": two long notes, then a run of triplets, often from low in a bush.

The song sparrow is busier: three or four sharp notes, then a jumbled trill, like someone clearing their throat and then rushing a sentence. A trick from the Maplefield Bird Club: the whistle that sounds like a question is the white-throat; the one that sounds like an argument is the song sparrow.

## Science fiction books / serial: The book that was written on a lighthouse

Maya reads science fiction; this is the invented story behind a classic she may not know, told in five beats.

Beat one. In 1962 a schoolteacher named Edith Varn took a winter job keeping a lighthouse on a rocky island off the northern coast. She brought a typewriter, two boxes of paper and one idea: a city built inside a hollowed-out comet, where nobody had seen a sky in three hundred years.

Beat two. The keeper's log survives. Most days read "wind northwest, lamp lit, 4 pages." The storms cut the island off for 41 days that winter, and Varn wrote that the isolation was the point. She wanted to know what people forget when they can't see outside, so she stopped looking out herself.

Beat three. Her first draft was 600 pages and her publisher said no. Too slow, too quiet, nobody fights anybody. Varn cut it in half on the ferry home, throwing pages over the rail as she went, keeping only the chapters where the comet's children first argue about whether the sky is real.

Beat four. "The Comet Children" came out in 1964 to almost no reviews. It sold 3,000 copies. Then a radio station read it aloud, one chapter a night, during a long winter in 1971, and the letters started: people said they had been waiting by the radio the way the comet's children waited for a window.

Beat five. Varn never wrote another novel. She went back to teaching and said the lighthouse had used up her one big idea. The book has never gone out of print, and the island's lighthouse, now automated, has a small plaque by the door: a woman wrote a sky here.
