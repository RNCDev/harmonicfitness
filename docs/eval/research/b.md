## Deep tech / big_story: Corvane moves the power lines under the chip

Theo likes the technical detail, and this is the biggest chip news of the week.

Corvane Semiconductor, an invented chipmaker, announced on Tuesday that its next process will feed power from the back of the silicon wafer instead of the front. Today, on almost every chip, the tiny wires carrying power and the wires carrying signals are tangled together in the same dozen-plus layers on top of the transistors.

Moving power underneath frees the top layers for signals alone. Corvane says its test chips lose 30 percent less voltage on the way to the transistors and pack logic 11 percent tighter. The catch: the wafer has to be flipped, bonded to a carrier and thinned to a few micrometers, about a twentieth of a human hair, without cracking.

What's next: Corvane expects first customer chips in late 2027. Analysts quoted in the announcement say yield is the whole question; if too many thinned wafers crack, the gains vanish in the price.

## Deep tech / explainer: Why chipmakers use light you can't see

The follow-on to Corvane, for a set's worth of thinking.

Chip patterns are printed with light through a stencil, like a slide projector in reverse. The finer the pattern, the shorter the light's wavelength has to be. Visible light is around 400 to 700 nanometers; the smallest features on a modern chip are far smaller than that.

So the newest machines use extreme ultraviolet light at 13.5 nanometers. Air absorbs it, glass absorbs it, so everything happens in a vacuum and the light bounces off mirrors instead of passing through lenses. The light comes from droplets of molten tin, each hit twice by a laser, about 50,000 times a second.

Source: https://example.org/euv-explained (2026-10-20)

## Learning Italian / language_bite: Piano piano si va lontano

Theo wants a phrase a session; this one suits a workout.

"Piano piano si va lontano": slowly, slowly, you go far. "Piano" here means slowly or softly, the same word as on sheet music, and "lontano" is far away. Italians say it to someone rushing a hard job. It fits the last set of anything: steady beats fast.
